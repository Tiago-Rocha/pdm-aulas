Store consoles and sprint 3 review

Theory (30 minutes): Play Console and App Store Connect: app listing, privacy declarations, tracks, staged rollout and review. Practice: last sprint review, final retrospective and list of pending items for the freeze on 09/12.

Freeze: 09/12 at 18:30
Open your Play Console and your App Store Connect before the lesson, already signed in, on a real app of yours. Today's theory is almost all shown live: each slide corresponds to a screen of the console. Confirm that every group brings the phone with the release build installed, not the debug one.

By the end of this lesson you can

  • Describe what an app listing needs in the Play Console and in App Store Connect, and what the privacy declarations require.
  • Tell the testing tracks from the production track, and explain what a staged rollout is for.
  • Respond to a review rejection methodically.
  • Read ratings, reviews and vitals after publishing, and say what they do to the app's visibility.
  • Present your group's sprint 3 and leave with the list of pending items closed for the freeze.

Play Console: listing and data safety (1 of 2)

  • Store listing: name (30 characters), short description (80), long description (4000), 512×512 icon, 1024×500 feature graphic, at least two phone screenshots, category, contact e-mail and privacy policy URL. The policy is mandatory for every app.
  • Data safety form: what data you collect, which you share, for what purpose, whether it travels encrypted, whether the user can ask for it to be deleted. It appears on the listing before installing. You also have to declare what the SDKs collect: Crashlytics collects device identifiers and crash logs.
  • Content rating: IARC questionnaire that generates the rating per region (PEGI in Europe). Without it the app does not get published.
  • Developer account: 25 USD once; Google verifies your identity and, for personal accounts, may require a closed testing phase with testers before production.

For the tests: the data safety form declares what the app and its SDKs collect; lying in it is grounds for removal.

Play Console: tracks, rollout and pre-launch (2 of 2)

TrackWho installsWhat for
Internal testingUp to 100 e-mails, no review, available in minutesThe team and the class
Closed testingLists or Google groups, with reviewPrivate beta; mandatory for new accounts
Open testingAnyone, through the listingPublic beta, feedback at scale
ProductionEveryoneWith a staged rollout: 1%, 5%, 20%, 50%, 100%
  • The staged rollout is there to catch a crash with 1% of users instead of 100%. You can stop (halt), but not go back: the fix is a new versionCode.
  • The pre-launch report runs the app on real Google devices, takes screenshots and reports crashes and accessibility problems before you publish.
  • Play review takes from hours to a few days; sensitive permissions (background location, SMS) take longer and ask for a video.
Analogy The staged rollout is opening the shop first to 5% of the customers and looking at the complaints before opening to everyone. If the vitals get worse, you close the door (halt) and fix it with a new versionCode; the other 95% never got to see the problem.
Show in your console: Release → Testing → Internal testing, with the list of testers and the opt-in link. Then Production → Releases, with a rollout at 20%. If you have an old pre-launch report, show the automatic screenshots: students like seeing the app being used by a robot.

App Store Connect: listing, privacy labels and screenshots

  • Listing: name (30), subtitle (30), description (4000), keywords (100 characters, comma-separated), support URL and privacy policy URL, category, age rating questionnaire.
  • Screenshots per device: a set for the 6.9-inch iPhone is mandatory; 13-inch iPad if the app runs on iPad. The images have to be of the real app, not mock-ups.
  • Privacy nutrition labels: three boxes on the listing, "Data used to track you", "Data linked to you" and "Data not linked to you". You fill them in by data category and purpose, including what the SDKs do.
  • Privacy manifest (PrivacyInfo.xcprivacy): since 2024 the SDKs and the app declare the sensitive APIs they use and why. Without it, the upload is rejected.
  • The listing and the build are separate things: you can have the listing ready weeks before the first build.

For the tests: Apple's privacy labels and Google's data safety are the same question in two forms: what you collect, what for, and whether it is linked to the person.

App Store Connect: review notes and what the review checks

  • Review notes: demo account with credentials, how to reach each feature, what is needed to test (an NFC tag, a location in the Azores). If it needs hardware, attach a video. Half of all rejections are avoided here.
  • What they check: crashes, incomplete functionality or test content, mandatory login for no reason, metadata that does not match the app, privacy, payments outside the store's system, apps that are "just a website".
  • Time: most reviews close within 24 to 48 hours. The first submission of a new app takes longer.
  • Expedited review: requested through a form for critical fixes or events with a date. It works when the reason is real; abusing it closes the door.
  • Google's review is more automatic, Apple's is more human. In both there are numbered guidelines: the rejection quotes the number.
Analogy Both stores are customs offices, but with different inspections. Apple's is human: someone opens the app, taps the buttons and reads the review notes, which is why it takes a day or two and quotes the rule that failed. Google's is mostly by machine: faster, but it catches what can be measured automatically (data safety, target API level, permissions) and does not forgive badly filled-in forms.

Responding to a rejection

  1. Read the guideline quoted, in full, on Apple's or Google's website. The rejection message summarises; the guideline explains.
  2. Reply in the Resolution Center (Apple) or in the appeal form (Google) if you think the reviewer did not understand. Short, factual, with screenshots or video. Many rejections are resolved with an explanation.
  3. Fix and resubmit if the problem is real. A new build, with a new build number, and notes saying what changed.
  4. Appeal to the App Review Board or to Play support only when the reply and the fix were not enough. It is slow.
  5. Do not upload the same build hoping for another reviewer, and do not argue: the goal is to publish, not to be right.
Real example "Guideline 2.1: we were unable to log in." The right response is not to argue: it is a demo account that works and a 30-second video. Published the next day.
Tell a real rejection of your own, with the original message and the reply you gave. The one that usually pays off most is a metadata one (a screenshot with a button that no longer existed) or a 4.2 for minimum functionality. Stress that the reviewer is a person with a list, not an enemy.

After publishing: ratings, reviews and vitals

  • Ratings and reviews: reply in public, politely, to all the ones that say something concrete. Ask for a rating inside the app at the right moment (in_app_review), never at launch. Play gives more weight to recent ratings.
  • Android vitals: Play measures the user-perceived crash rate and ANR rate. Above the thresholds (about 1.1% for crashes and 0.5% for ANRs) the app loses visibility in the store and gets a warning on its listing. This is a real penalty, not a suggestion.
  • App Store: metrics for crashes, retention and listing conversion in App Analytics; symbolicated crashes appear in the Xcode Organizer.
  • Adoption per version: how many users are still on the previous version. It decides when you can switch off an old API (lesson 28) or force an update (lesson 30).
  • Staged rollout + vitals is the cycle: raise the percentage when the numbers hold, stop when they do not.

For the tests: the stores penalise the visibility of apps that exceed the crash and ANR thresholds; publishing is the start of maintenance, not the end.

Sprint 3 review: the format

MinuteWhat the group does
0 to 1Goal of the sprint in two sentences and what got done vs what was planned (from the issue board, not from memory).
1 to 4Demo on the projected phone, release build: a complete user flow, including airplane mode and an error state.
4 to 5One technical problem you solved this sprint and how. A different person from the one who gave the demo.
+2Feedback from the teacher and from colleagues: one good thing, one thing to fix before the freeze.
  • Order: the one from today's draw on arrival. Whoever is not ready when called goes to the end.
  • It is the last review before the defences. What you hear here is what you will hear on the 14th if you do not fix it.
Stopwatch visible to everyone. Cut at 5 minutes with no exceptions, it is training for the defence. Note down the two feedback points per group on a sheet shared with the class; it is the basis for the list of pending items on the next slide. Check that the demo is the release build: if the debug banner shows up, ask why.

Final retro: if you started again

  1. Five minutes in silence. Each person writes on a sticky note (or in an issue) one thing they would do differently since the kickoff and one thing they would repeat exactly as it was.
  2. Five minutes in the group. Read them, group them, choose one of each.
  3. One minute per group to the class. The "would do differently" one and the "would repeat" one.
  • The ones that always come up: "start the tests earlier", "smaller PRs", "do not leave offline for the end", "agree on who does what before opening the editor".
  • The retro is not for blaming anyone. It is so that the next team you are in, at a company, does not repeat the same thing.
Task Each group pastes the two chosen sentences into the README, in a "Retrospective" section. It is part of the submission and says a lot about the team to whoever reads the repository.

List of pending items for the freeze on 09/12

  • Create a "Freeze v1.0.0" milestone on GitHub and put in it only the issues that fit in five working days. The rest goes into a "Later" milestone and is written in the README as future work.
  • Priority: minimum requirements working, then error and offline states, then green CI, then README and distribution, only then extras. Cut features, not quality.
  • Each issue with an owner and an associated PR. An issue with no owner on Friday is an issue that is not going to get done.
  • Friday (lesson 33) is the finishing workshop: bring the list already made, it is not for making it there.
  • Wednesday 09/12 at 18:30: tag v1.0.0. From then on only the README can change.
Task Before leaving the room, each group shows the teacher the "Freeze v1.0.0" milestone with the issues, the owners and the two feedback points from the review in it.
Go past each group in the last 20 minutes and look at the milestone with them. The usual mistake is an optimistic list: 15 issues for 5 days with test 2 in the middle. Help them cut. A group that reaches the freeze with the minimums working and an honest README gets a better mark than one with the map half done and favourites failing.
