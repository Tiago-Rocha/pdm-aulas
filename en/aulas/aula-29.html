Stores and monetisation, and sprint 3

How many apps reach the stores, how many make money and how they make it. After that, the rest of the lesson is for sprint 3, in the group project.

Sprint 3 review: Wednesday 02/12 at 16:30
Plan: 08:30 to 09:05 theory; 09:05 to 09:15 sprint check-in; 09:15 to 10:30 project work, with the teacher going round the groups. To wake the room up: "who paid for an app this year, and how much?". Almost only subscriptions come up (Spotify, YouTube Premium, Duolingo, iCloud) and hardly any one-off purchase. That is the lesson summed up by them.

By the end of this lesson you can

  • Say how many apps try to get into the stores and why most never make money.
  • Tell apart the business models of an app, with examples you use.
  • Know when a payment must go through the store and how much the store keeps.
  • Understand how the top earning apps make money, and why Uber pays nothing to the store.
Before moving on, ask and write the bets on the board: "how many apps are submitted to the App Store per day? 100, 1,000 or 10,000?" and "out of every four, how many does Apple reject?". Answers on the next slide: close to 25,000 per day, and one in four.

What to say: 9.1 million divided by 365 is close to 25,000 per day. Each submission is a version: a new app or an update, and the same app can be rejected and come back several times. Sensor Tower counts even more new apps, close to 600 thousand in 2025. Apple has also been removing "vibe coding" apps that generate other apps inside them (Replit, Vibecode, Anything) for breaking the rules. The message: making an app got easy, getting someone to find it and pay for it did not.

What to say: the top rejection reason is nothing sophisticated, it is the app crashing or being half done (guideline 2.1). Hence "test on a freshly installed phone" and the demo account in the notes for the reviewer. The 12 testers rule exists since November 2023 for personal accounts and is what most delays a first solo launch: people sell "12 testers" on Fiverr, and Google detects those who install and do not use the app. Company accounts do not have this rule, but they need a D-U-N-S number. What you already did with Firebase App Distribution and the 3 testers is the practice run for this.

And how many make money

17 out of every 100 new subscription apps reach $1,000 a month in their first two years.

  • In January 2026 alone, 14,700 new subscription apps came out, seven times more than in 2022.
  • 69 % of revenue goes to apps launched before 2020. Apps launched in 2025 get 3 %.
  • One year after launch, the average app makes about $72 a month. The top 10 % make over $2,500.
  • The winners are the ones already there: a winner-takes-all market.

Source: RevenueCat, State of Subscription Apps 2026 (over 115,000 apps and 16 billion dollars in revenue).

What to say: RevenueCat is the service many apps use to manage subscriptions, so it sees the real revenue of tens of thousands of apps. Before talking about the figure, ask "out of 100 apps, how many reach a thousand dollars a month?": most bet high. A thousand dollars a month, before taxes and the store's commission, does not even pay a salary. The sentence to take home: there are more apps than ever and revenue is concentrated in the old ones (Spotify, Duolingo, Tinder, Calm). If they ask why: brand, user base and years of paywall testing.

What to say: before showing the last column, ask "how much does Apple make from each Uber ride?". Answer: nothing. Uber: bookings are what customers pay; revenue (52 billion) is what Uber keeps after paying drivers and restaurants, about 27 %. Uber One is a subscription, but it gives discounts on rides and deliveries, which are real services, so it does not pay the store either; members already make up almost half of bookings. Bolt: a company from Estonia; Markus Villig started at 19 with €5,000 borrowed from his family, signing up Tallinn taxi drivers one by one; it is one of the most used apps in Portugal. Ryanair: the fiscal year ends in March; extras bring €24 per passenger and are sold almost entirely in the app and on the website, along the trip (bag, seat, priority), with no store commission; the boarding pass lives in the app. ChatGPT and Claude: the Appfigures numbers are estimates and only count what was paid through the App Store and Google Play; most subscriptions are paid on the web. In an AI chat every message costs money in servers, so the store's cut hurts; in the US, since 2025, they can send people to pay on the website. ChatGPT makes more than twice all its rivals combined, but the gap is shrinking.

What to say: "who has access to what" is called an entitlement. The app does not ask "did this person pay?", it asks "does this person have Pro?". Enterprise apps are distributed through Apple Business Manager or managed Google Play, without going through the public store. For Tempo Açores, with public data, the natural model is free, or freemium with an extra worth paying for (real-time warnings per island, 10-day forecast). What you charge for has to be worth more than the hassle of paying.

Four stories

Flappy Bird 2014

$50,000 a day

One developer on his own in Vietnam, ads only. At its peak, he pulled the game from the stores: "it ruins my simple life".

Wordle 2022

Over $1 million

Made for his partner, no ads and no purchases. The New York Times bought it and put it in its games subscription.

Cal AI 2024 to 2026

$30 million a year

Two high school students: snap the plate and AI counts the calories. Subscription, 15 million downloads. Bought by MyFitnessPal.

Fortnite 2020 to 2025

5 years out

Epic added a cheaper direct payment inside the game. Apple pulled it from the store within hours. It only came back in the US in 2025, after the courts.

What to say, one lesson per story. Flappy Bird: ads only pay with huge volume (50 million downloads); the figure was given by the author, Dong Nguyen, in an interview, it is not audited; the stores filled up with copies. Wordle: Josh Wardle made the game for his partner; the NYT paid "low seven figures" and the game became the entry point to the paper's games subscription: the money was with whoever bought it, not in the game. Cal AI: Zach Yadegari and Henry Langmack started in high school; a simple idea, a camera, an AI API and a paywall; the sale to MyFitnessPal was announced in March 2026, price not disclosed. Fortnite: the store's rules matter more than the size of the company; it led to lawsuits in the US and the EU that changed the payment rules (next slide).

For a small project: 15 % on Apple; on Google 25 % on purchases and 15 % on subscriptions. Alternative payments only with an accountant next to you.

What to say: until 2025 it was simple, 30 % on both, 15 % for small developers. The table shows the EU, which is where you publish. Apple, since 1 October 2026: 26 % with Apple's payment system; 15 % for the Small Business Program (under 1 million dollars a year) and for subscriptions from each subscriber's second year; with alternative payment, 20 % (10 % for small developers); with a link to the website, 15 % (10 %). Google, since 30 June 2026 in the EU, the UK and the US: 20 % on purchases from new installs and 25 % from existing installs, 10 % on subscriptions, plus 5 % if Google's billing is used; in those regions the 15 % tier up to 1 million is gone, so a small project pays more on purchases than it used to. In the rest of the world the new fees arrive by September 2027. In the US, since the April 2025 ruling, Apple has to allow links to the website; the appeals court let it charge something and it asked for 15 % in August 2026, still undecided. Google stopped requiring its billing in the US on 29 October 2025. Sources: Apple Newsroom (August 2026) and Google Play Console Help (service fees). If a student asks for exact numbers: always check App Store Connect and the Play Console.

What to say: the same €20 note ends up in very different places depending on what is sold and where it is paid. On the ride the store does not see a cent, because it is a real world service. On the subscription bought on the phone Apple keeps more than €5 in year 1 (€3 from year 2, with the 15 %). On the website, the payment processor keeps a few cents. Netflix stopped selling in the iPhone app in 2018 and Spotify in 2016. Ask the room: "if the Tempo Açores app sold a Pro version, where would you put the pay button?". Answer: inside the app it pays commission but converts more, because it is one tap; on the website people drop off along the way. It is a trade off, there is no right answer.

The rules change every year

2008

App Store and Android Market open: 30 % for everyone.

2011

Apple bans links to buy outside. Kindle removes the store from its app.

2016

Apple: subscriptions at 15 % after year 1. Google follows in 2018.

2020

Fortnite adds its own payment and both stores kick it out on the same day.

2021

15 % up to $1 M a year: Apple in January, Google in July.

2024 EU

Digital Markets Act: alternative payments and stores. A €1.8 billion fine for Apple.

2025 US

Links to the website with no commission: Apple in May, Google in October. Fortnite returns.

2026 Google

June: 20 % on purchases, 10 % on subscriptions, plus 5 % for billing.

2026 Apple

October: 26 % in the EU. In the US it asks for 15 % on links; the court decides.

2027 World

September: Google's new fees reach every country.

more room for app makers tighter rule still coming

What to say: seventeen years of rules and almost every change came from outside the stores: courts, the European Commission and lawsuits like Epic's. The lesson: a business model that only works with today's fees is fragile; whoever sells digital content has to redo the maths every year. Detail for whoever asks: in 2011 Apple started requiring subscriptions to go through the store and banned links to pay outside; Amazon responded by removing the store from the Kindle iPhone app. In 2016 Apple moved subscriptions to 85 % for the developer after year 1; Google did the same in January 2018. In August 2020 Epic added a direct payment to Fortnite and both stores removed it on the same day. The Digital Markets Act (DMA) applies since March 2024; the 1.8 billion fine is from March 2024, after a complaint by Spotify; another one, of 500 million, came in April 2025. In the US, the judge ruled in April 2025 that Apple could not charge on links; in December 2025 the appeals court let it charge something, and in August 2026 Apple proposed 15 % (5 % for small developers). Google stopped requiring its billing in the US on 29 October 2025, by court order in the Epic case. Google's dates for 2026 and 2027 come from its March 2026 announcement.

In the app: the store charges, someone confirms

// Which plans exist? Created in the store console
final offerings = await Purchases.getOfferings();

// On any screen: does this person have Pro?
final info = await Purchases.getCustomerInfo();

in_app_purchaseOfficial plugin: talks to both stores; validating the purchase is up to you.
RevenueCatValidates on the server and answers "has Pro?". Free to start.

Server-side validation is a must: on a rooted phone, a fake purchase takes minutes.

What to say: the app never decides on its own whether someone paid. The store returns a receipt (Apple) or a purchase token (Google) and a server confirms it with the App Store Server API or the Play Developer API. The server also gets the renewal, failed payment and refund notifications, and updates access. RevenueCat runs that server for you; with in_app_purchase you have to build it. To buy with RevenueCat: Purchases.purchase(...) with the chosen plan; the result brings the new CustomerInfo. Products (id, price tier, name in each language) are created in App Store Connect and the Play Console. There is no exercise on this in the project: the course API does not sell anything.
