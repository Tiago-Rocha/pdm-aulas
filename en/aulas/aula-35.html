How to defend a project, freeze and rehearsal

Theory: structure of a ten-minute presentation, live demonstration on the phone, plan B, and the questions where each member answers for what they did. Practice: project freeze with the tag v1.0.0 at 18:30, draw for the order of the defences and rehearsal with feedback from classmates.

Freeze at 18:30
Set up scrcpy on your laptop and bring a USB-C cable and a Lightning cable to lend. Test the projector with a phone before the lesson: half of the problems in the defences are the projection. Bring a list of the groups for the draw and a visible timer. Hand out the test 2 marks at the end, not at the start, so they do not steal attention from the lesson.

By the end of this lesson you can

  • Structure a 10-minute technical presentation with three slides and a live demo.
  • Project the phone in the room and have a plan B that starts in 20 seconds.
  • Anticipate the typical questions of a defence and answer for the code you signed.
  • Close the project with the tag v1.0.0 and know what can still change afterwards.
  • Give and receive useful feedback in a 5-minute rehearsal.

Structure: ten minutes, five parts

MinutePartWhat to say
0 to 1ProblemWho the app is for and what it solves. One sentence. It is not "we made a weather app": it is "people who live in Horta want to know whether there is a boat tomorrow".
1 to 2What you builtThe minimums, the extras, what was left out and why. Honesty here avoids bad questions later.
2 to 6DemoA real user using the app: search, forecast, favourite on another phone, airplane mode, a handled error. Four minutes, rehearsed to the second.
6 to 9Technical decisionsThree decisions and the why: architecture, cache, what you did when the API changed. With a diagram, not with code on the screen.
9 to 10What you would do differentlyOne concrete thing you learned. It closes well and shows maturity.

Three slides at most

  • Slide 1: the app. Name, the members, one screenshot per main screen. It stays on screen while you explain the problem and what you built.
  • Slide 2: the architecture. A diagram of boxes: UI → notifier → repository → API and cache. Arrows with names. It is the slide that supports the technical decisions and the answers to the questions.
  • Slide 3: what you learned. Three bullets: what you would do differently, what was left for later, what each of you took away. It stays projected during the questions.
  • No slides of code. If a question asks for code, open the editor and show the file: it is more credible and proves that you know where it is.
  • No reading. Anyone who reads slides is telling the panel that they do not know what they did. Keywords on the slide, sentences in your mouth.

For the tests: there are none. This is for life: in technical interviews they will ask you for exactly this, "tell me about a project of yours in five minutes".

Live demo: phone projected

  • Android: scrcpy on the laptop, phone on a cable with USB debugging switched on. It shows the phone's screen in a window that you project. Install it today and test it today, not on Monday.
  • iPhone: on a Mac, QuickTime → New Movie Recording → choose the iPhone as the camera; or AirPlay mirroring if the room has it. On Windows there is no reliable way: record a video.
  • Prepared data: favourites already chosen, cache already filled, the location for the demo chosen beforehand. Nobody wants to watch you type "Ponta Delgada" with the keyboard shaking.
  • Airplane mode rehearsed: know exactly which screen shows "data from 12 min ago" and how long it takes to appear.
  • Notifications and calls switched off on the demo phone. Do Not Disturb mode. Brightness at maximum.
  • The person doing the demo is not the person speaking: one handles the phone, the other narrates. Rehearse the choreography.
Demonstrate scrcpy now with a student's phone: 30 seconds to install with brew or winget, plug in the cable, run. Show what happens when USB debugging is not authorised (black screen): it is what will happen to someone on Monday.

Plan B: the video

  • Record today or tomorrow, with the app already at the freeze, the same path as the demo: 3 to 4 minutes, with the phone's screen recorded (Android: native screen recording or scrcpy --record demo.mp4; iPhone: screen recording from Control Centre).
  • No ambient sound. Whoever presents narrates over the video, as they would with the real demo.
  • It lives on the laptop and on a USB stick and at a link. The day the projector does not recognise the laptop is the day of the defence.
  • If the live demo fails, do not insist for more than 20 seconds: "I will switch to the video" and carry on. The panel values those who manage the problem, not those who get flustered.
  • Having a plan B is not a sign of weakness; it is what a professional team does before any presentation to a client.
The emulator is not plan B Showing the app on the laptop's emulator does not count as a demonstration. The rubric asks for the app on the phone; the video is of the phone.

Typical questions (1 of 2)

QuestionA good answer includes
Why this architecture?What you gained (testing without network, swapping the source), what it cost (more files), and which alternative you considered.
What happens without network?Cache-first, what is shown, how the age of the data is marked, what happens to a favourite added offline.
Where is the token?flutter_secure_storage, Keychain and Keystore, why not in shared_preferences, how it is renewed on a 401.
How did you test?How many tests, of what kind, with which fakes, what the CI runs on each PR, and what is not tested.
Show me where that is in the code.Opening the right file in less than 15 seconds. If you hesitate, you do not know where it lives.

Typical questions (2 of 2): how to answer

  • Each member answers for what they did. The questions are addressed to one person by name, based on their PRs. If your teammate answers for you, it counts against both of you.
  • Short answer first, detail afterwards. "Cache-first with drift; I show the old data with the time and refresh in the background" and then, if they ask, the rest.
  • "I don't know" is acceptable once, followed by "but I know where I would look" or "what I would do is". Making things up is not acceptable even once.
  • Questions about what you did not do: "we did not do it because X; if we had one more sprint we would do Y like this". No excuses, with a plan.
  • Test 2 questions applied to the project: count on them. "Which location permission do you request and why?", "What does your WorkManager guarantee?", "What happens if you reuse the versionCode?".
  • The panel is not trying to catch you out; it is trying to work out how much of the code is yours. Make its life easy.
Run a 2-minute simulation with a volunteer: pick one of their PRs on GitHub, live, and ask "explain this line to me". It is uncomfortable and it is exactly what will happen on Monday. End by saying that those who were in the PRs have nothing to worry about.

What the rubric rewards and penalises in the defence

Rewards

  • App running on the phone, release build, no debug banner.
  • Technical decisions explained with the why and the alternative.
  • Each member answering for their own code, with the PR history confirming it.
  • Error and offline states shown without the panel asking.
  • Honesty about what is missing and what you would do differently.

Penalises

  • Reading slides. Slides with code. More than three slides.
  • One member speaking for everyone, or answering for the others.
  • Going over 10 minutes: the demo is cut wherever it is.
  • Not knowing where something is in your own code.
  • Demo on the emulator, or a debug build "because the release one would not install".

Distribution and presentation are worth 10% of the project; the individual defence adjusts each person's mark. It is all in Assessment.

Freeze: tag v1.0.0 at 18:30

  1. By 18:15: all the PRs in the "Freeze v1.0.0" milestone merged or closed. What does not make it goes to "Later" and to the README.
  2. Green CI on main. Without it there is no tag; fix what fails or revert the PR.
  3. Version: version: 1.0.0+N in pubspec.yaml, with the N of the last build distributed.
  4. Create and publish the tag from an up-to-date main. The teacher checks the time of the commit the tag points to. 
    git checkout main
git pull
git tag -a v1.0.0 -m "Tempo Açores v1.0.0: submission for the defence"
git push origin v1.0.0
  5. From 18:30 onwards only README.md can change (screenshots, links, retrospective). Any other commit after the tag is not assessed and counts as an attempt to get round the deadline.
Task Tag published and release link on Moodle by 18:30. Confirm with git ls-remote --tags origin that the tag is on GitHub, not only on the laptop.
At 18:30 sharp, open each group's GitHub and record the tag's commit on a sheet of paper. If a group asks for "just five more minutes", the answer is no: it is one of the few things in this course with a fixed time, and that is the lesson.

Draw for the order of the defences

  • The groups are drawn now, in the room, with a random number generator projected. The order goes on Moodle today.
  • Monday 14/12, 16:00 to 18:00: first half of the groups. Wednesday 16/12, 08:30 to 10:30: the rest, followed by the wrap-up.
  • Each group has 10 minutes of presentation, 5 of questions and 2 of feedback. Between groups, 1 minute to swap the phone on the projector.
  • All members present at their own defence, and all groups present on both days: watching the others defend is part of the learning, and there is feedback from classmates.
  • Swaps of position only by agreement between two groups, communicated to the teacher by Friday.
Order Whoever goes first has less time to wait and a fresher panel. It is not a punishment.

Rehearsal: five minutes per group

  1. Compressed version of the defence: 1 minute of problem and what you built, 2 minutes of demo on the projected phone, 1 minute on one technical decision, 1 minute of questions from a classmate in another group.
  2. Visible timer. At 5 minutes the teacher cuts you off, even in the middle of a sentence. That is what will happen on Monday at 10.
  3. Real projection: the phone connected to the room's projector, not to the group's laptop. If it fails now, great: it fails today and not on Monday.
  4. A classmate from another group asks one question from the list of typical questions, addressed to a member of their choice.
  5. 2 minutes of feedback with the grid on the next slide, in writing, handed to the group.
Task All groups rehearse today, in the order of the draw. Anyone whose demo fails on the projector sorts it out before leaving the room, with the teacher's help.

Collect the grids and return them to the respective group at the end. Read the free sentences yourself before handing them over: if two or three repeat for the same group, tell them aloud. It is the feedback they are most likely to follow.
