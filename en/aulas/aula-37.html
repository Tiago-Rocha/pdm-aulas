What the rubric rewards, in short

  • Functionality: the minimums working in the demo, extras shown and not just announced.
  • Quality and architecture: decisions with the why and the rejected alternative; knowing where each thing lives in the code; green CI on the tag v1.0.0.
  • User experience: loading, error and empty states shown without being asked; airplane mode with old data marked.
  • Process: each member answers for their PRs; the history has already told most of the story.
  • Distribution and presentation: signed build on the testers' phones; 10 minutes well used; short, concrete answers.

Today's groups had two extra days and saw six defences. The panel expects that to show.

Keep exactly the same standard as on Monday, including the cut at 10 minutes. Today's groups have the advantage of having seen the others; the only way to be fair to Monday's groups is not to go easy today.

Typical questions: what each member should expect

  • About your PR: one question with your code projected. "Explain this line to me", "why did you not use X?".
  • About resilience: no network, 429, 500, unexpected JSON; what the user sees in each case.
  • About security: where the token is, what is in the repository that should not be there, which permissions you request and when.
  • About tests and CI: what is tested, what is not, what runs on each PR.
  • About the test 2 material: the three questions most often missed, applied to your code. Those who were at the commented correction know which they are.
  • About what comes next: "what was missing to put this on the Play Store?", with the answer going through the store listing, data safety, content rating and tracks from lesson 32.

Looking back: what this course covered

  • What makes mobile different: small screen, battery, intermittent network, imposed lifecycle, sandbox and permissions, stores as the gatekeepers of publication. It was lesson 1 and it came back in all the others.
  • Flutter and Dart: widgets, state, navigation, layouts, themes, i18n, accessibility, tests. One codebase for two platforms, and hot reload as a way of learning.
  • A real app from end to end: API with authentication, offline cache, resilience to failures, location and map, notifications, deep links, background, native channels.
  • What surrounds the code: architecture, git in a team, PRs, CI, observability, security and privacy, build and signing, distribution to testers, stores.
  • Process: three sprints, reviews, retros, a freeze with a fixed time, a defence. It was the part most like a job.

37 lessons, 81 hours, one app installed on someone else's phone.

What was left out, and what you only learn with practice

  • We could not fit in: advanced animations, Cupertino and apps that follow iOS to the millimetre, home screen widgets and Live Activities, wearables, desktop and web with Flutter, gRPC and GraphQL in practice, E2E tests with Patrol or Maestro, code push in production.
  • You only learn it with time: maintaining an app for two years, migrating between Flutter versions, replying to a one-star review, an app with 100,000 users failing on a phone model you have never seen.
  • Not Flutter: native Swift and Kotlin are still valuable, and an experienced mobile developer reads both. What you learned about lifecycle, permissions, stores and signing is the same there.
  • What changes fast: packages, versions, the UI of the consoles. What does not change: the network fails, the battery runs out, the user does not read, the store rejects.
Five minutes for the class Each person says in one sentence what they found hardest and what will be most useful to them. The teacher takes notes: this is how the next edition gets better.
Let them speak; do not comment on each sentence. Note the answers down in a list where everyone can see it. At the end say what you would change in the course, honestly, and what turned out better than you expected. The credibility of the look back comes from admitting the failings aloud.

Next steps: portfolio and publishing the app

  1. The repository is the portfolio. README with screenshots, a GIF of the app, architecture in ten lines and your name on the PRs. Make it public if the group agrees; a recruiter opens the README and decides in two minutes.
  2. A project that is yours alone, small and finished, is worth more than three half done. An app that solves a problem of yours, published, with 20 real users, is a whole interview.
  3. Publish. Google Play account (25 USD, once): store listing, data safety, content rating, testers, production. You already know how to do all of this. The App Store costs 99 USD per year and a Mac; it makes sense when you have something that justifies it.
  4. Maintain. Update Flutter when a version comes out, reply to the reviews, look at the vitals. Six months of a maintained app teach more than any course.
  5. Write about what you learned: a post about chaos mode and retry, or about the migration to API v2. It shows that you can explain, which is half of the job.

Next steps: communities and jobs

  • Communities: the community page at flutter.dev/community lists the official Discord, r/FlutterDev and the meetups. In Portugal, the Flutter Portugal groups and the GDGs (Google Developer Groups) in Lisbon and Porto hold regular meetups, almost always streamed. Going to one and asking a question is the step with the best return you can take this year.
  • Contributing: a bug reported with a reproduction in a pub.dev package, a documentation PR, a translation. It stays on your GitHub forever and teaches you to read other people's code.
  • Where the jobs are: LinkedIn with "Flutter" and "mobile" in Portugal and remote; itjobs.pt and landing.jobs; the careers pages of the companies I have already told you about that use Flutter in production. Many junior mobile openings ask for "Flutter or React Native or native": what you learned serves for all three.
  • What mobile interviews ask: lifecycle, state, what happens without network, where you store a token, how you test, how you publish. It is the syllabus of this course. Take the project with you and show it on the phone.
  • The teacher stays reachable at the institutional email address for questions, letters of recommendation and to hear that the app is in the store.
Show your app in the Play Console and in App Store Connect for 2 minutes: the vitals, a review you replied to, the rollout. It is the proof that the path you described exists. End with your contact details projected and thank them for the semester, by name if you can.
