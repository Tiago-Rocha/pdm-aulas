Why distribute early

  • Real feedback. The tester does not know where to tap. You do, and that is why you never find the problem.
  • Real installation. A release build, no cable, on a phone that is not yours, with another Android version, another screen size and another keyboard.
  • Real crashes. With Wednesday's Crashlytics or Sentry, ten testers for a week give you more information than a hundred hours of emulator.
  • The big apps do this at scale: Google Maps has a beta programme on Play, Instagram on TestFlight, with thousands of testers before each version.
  • Cost: one morning to set up, five minutes per build afterwards. Without this, the first tester is the customer.

For the tests: distributing early gives real feedback, installation and crashes; the tester does not know where to tap, and that is why they are useful.

Analogy Distributing to testers is the preview screening of a film for an invited audience, before the premiere. Firebase App Distribution and TestFlight are the preview cinema: no box office, only invited people get in, and the comments come back to the team in time to fix things. The premiere is the store, and there the first viewer has already paid for a ticket.
Tell your own story of a bug that only showed up on a tester's phone. Ask: "how many of you have installed your group's app on the phone of someone who is not in the group?" Usually nobody. Today that changes.

Firebase App Distribution (1 of 2)

  • A free Firebase service for delivering Android (APK or AAB) and iOS (IPA) builds to testers, without going through the store.
  • Testers by email, organised into groups ("daw-class", "qa", "client"). They get an email with a link, install the App Tester app on Android, and see every available version.
  • Android: an APK installs directly (the tester allows "install from unknown sources" once). An AAB forces you to link the account to Play to generate the APKs. For the class, APK.
  • iOS: needs an IPA signed with an ad hoc profile that includes the UDID of each iPhone, or an enterprise account. That is why, for iOS, TestFlight is simpler.
  • Each release has release notes, and the tester can send feedback with a screenshot from inside the app itself.

For the tests: App Distribution delivers APKs to testers by email, with no store; on iOS it requires an ad hoc profile with UDIDs, which makes TestFlight preferable.

Firebase App Distribution (2 of 2): console, CLI and Actions

  • Console: drag the APK, choose groups, write notes. Good for the first time, bad for the tenth.
  • CLI: firebase appdistribution:distribute app-release.apk --app ID --groups daw-class. One command, repeatable, fits in a script.
  • GitHub Actions: a job that runs flutter build apk --release and distributes on every tag or every merge into main. From then on, no production build leaves anyone's laptop.
  • CI authentication: a Firebase service account stored in GitHub Secrets. Never in the repository.
  • Alternatives: Diawi (a direct link to an APK), Play internal testing itself, or Codemagic with built-in distribution.

For the tests: distributing from CI guarantees reproducible builds; the Firebase credential goes in Secrets, never in the repository.

TestFlight

  • Apple's beta service: builds go up to App Store Connect and testers install through the TestFlight app.
  • Needs a paid account (99 USD per year), a Mac with Xcode to sign and upload, and a registered bundle id.
  • Internal testers: up to 100 team members in App Store Connect, no review, build available minutes after processing.
  • External testers: up to 10 000, by email or public link; the first build of each version goes through Beta App Review (hours to a day). Builds expire after 90 days.
  • Feedback: annotated screenshots and crash reports arrive in App Store Connect. Each build carries "What to test" notes.

For the tests: TestFlight requires a paid account and a Mac; internal up to 100 with no review, external up to 10 000 with Beta App Review; builds expire in 90 days.

Good distribution practices

  • Release notes on every build: what to test, what changed, what you already know is broken. "Build 47" with no notes is a build nobody tests.
  • Versioning: bump version: 1.2.0+47 in pubspec.yaml before every distribution. Two builds with the same number confuse testers and the store, and Play rejects them.
  • In-app feedback: a "Report a problem" button that opens email or the App Distribution feedback, with version and model pre-filled.
  • Never a debug build: it is 2 to 3 times slower and twice the size. The testers will say the app is slow, and they are right.
  • Early and often: one build a week with short notes is worth more than a "final" build the night before.

For the tests: never distribute debug builds; every build with a new version number and release notes.

Block 1: light and dark theme from a seed

import 'package:flutter/material.dart';

class AppTheme {
  static const _seed = Color(0xFF0B63C9);

  static ThemeData light() => _base(ColorScheme.fromSeed(seedColor: _seed));

  static ThemeData dark() =>
      _base(ColorScheme.fromSeed(seedColor: _seed, brightness: Brightness.dark));

  static ThemeData _base(ColorScheme scheme) => ThemeData(
        useMaterial3: true,
        colorScheme: scheme,
        appBarTheme: AppBarTheme(
          backgroundColor: scheme.surface,
          foregroundColor: scheme.onSurface,
          centerTitle: false,
        ),
        cardTheme: const CardThemeData(margin: EdgeInsets.symmetric(horizontal: 16, vertical: 6)),
        textTheme: const TextTheme(
          headlineSmall: TextStyle(fontWeight: FontWeight.w700, letterSpacing: -0.5),
          bodyMedium: TextStyle(height: 1.4),
        ),
      );
}

Block 1: hook it up to MaterialApp and use it in widgets

MaterialApp(
  theme: AppTheme.light(),
  darkTheme: AppTheme.dark(),
  themeMode: context.watch<SettingsNotifier>().themeMode, // system, light or dark
  // ...
)
// In a widget: never Colors.blue, always the theme's scheme
class WarningChip extends StatelessWidget {
  const WarningChip({super.key, required this.level});
  final WarningLevel level;

  @override
  Widget build(BuildContext context) {
    final scheme = Theme.of(context).colorScheme;
    final (background, text) = switch (level) {
      WarningLevel.red => (scheme.errorContainer, scheme.onErrorContainer),
      WarningLevel.orange || WarningLevel.yellow => (scheme.tertiaryContainer, scheme.onTertiaryContainer),
      WarningLevel.green => (scheme.secondaryContainer, scheme.onSecondaryContainer),
    };
    return Chip(
      label: Text(level.name, style: TextStyle(color: text)),
      backgroundColor: background,
    );
  }
}

Block 1: what matters

  • ColorScheme.fromSeed generates the roughly 30 Material 3 colours from a single one; the dark version comes from the same seed with Brightness.dark. Consistency for free.
  • Rule: no fixed colours in widgets. Colors.white on a Container stays white in the dark theme and is blinding. The pairs surface/onSurface, primaryContainer/onPrimaryContainer guarantee contrast.
  • Typography: define it in the textTheme and use Theme.of(context).textTheme.titleMedium. Do not repeat TextStyle(fontSize: 18) in twenty places.
  • ThemeMode.system by default; the toggle from lesson 9 saves the choice in shared_preferences.
  • Test both themes on every screen: the error state, the skeleton, the map (the OSM tiles are light; there are dark tiles).

Block 2: what matters

  • No strings in the UI. Every fixed Text('...') is an i18n bug waiting to happen. Extracting all the strings is laborious; that is why you do it early and not the night before.
  • Plurals with ICU syntax: =0, =1, other. Portuguese and English are simple; Russian and Arabic have more categories, and intl knows them.
  • The @key metadata is only mandatory in the template file; the translations only need the keys. A key missing from en: the generator warns and uses pt.
  • Dates with DateFormat and the active locale. DateTime.toString() in the UI is forbidden.
  • Layouts: "Try again" in German is "Erneut versuchen". Fixed-width buttons break. Test in English and imagine twice the length.
  • An extension extension L10n on BuildContext { AppLocalizations get l10n => AppLocalizations.of(this)!; } saves you the ! everywhere.

  • Source: a 1024×1024 PNG. iOS does not accept transparency (hence remove_alpha_ios). The Android adaptive icon has a separate background and foreground, and the launcher crops it (circle, squircle) however it likes: leave a margin in the foreground.

Block 4: release build and install on another group's phone

flutter build apk --release
ls -lh build/app/outputs/flutter-apk/app-release.apk
adb install -r build/app/outputs/flutter-apk/app-release.apk
  1. Bump the version first: version: 0.3.0+3 in pubspec.yaml. The +3 is Android's versionCode; it has to grow with every distributed build.
  2. Build: 2 to 4 minutes the first time. The APK ends up in build/app/outputs/flutter-apk/app-release.apk, at around 20 MB.
  3. Install on the phone of a colleague from another group: by cable with adb install, or send the file (Drive, Bluetooth, Nearby Share) and open it on their phone.
  4. Observe: ask them to use the app for two minutes with no help. Do not explain anything. Note down what they did not understand.

Block 4: what matters

  • Release: AOT, no asserts, no hot reload. Two to three times faster and half the size of debug. It is the only build you distribute.
  • Signing: by default build.gradle signs the release with the debug key. It installs, but Play does not accept it and every group shares the same key. The release key is covered in lesson 31.
  • --split-per-abi generates one APK per architecture (arm64-v8a, armeabi-v7a, x86_64), each a third of the size. For the store, flutter build appbundle.
  • Unknown sources: Android asks the tester whether they trust the source (Files, Chrome, App Tester). Once per source, not per app.
  • The client-id for the course API goes into the build through --dart-define, as in lesson 18. Confirm it is not in the repository before distributing.

Block 5: Firebase App Distribution in the console

  1. Project: at console.firebase.google.com, "Add project" with the name tempo-acores-groupNN. Analytics can stay switched off.
  2. Android app: "Add app" → Android, with the package name equal to the applicationId in android/app/build.gradle.kts (for example pt.uac.tempoacores.group01). The google-services.json is only needed if you use Crashlytics or Analytics.
  3. Distribute: "Run" menu → App Distribution → "Get started" → drag in the app-release.apk.
  4. Testers: "Testers and groups" tab → group daw-class with your colleagues' emails (the teacher shares the list on Moodle).
  5. Release: add the group, write notes ("v0.3.0: synced favourites and map. We know offline fails on the warnings screen.") and "Distribute".
  6. On the tester's side: email → accept the invitation → install App Tester → install the app. In the console you see who accepted and who installed.

Block 5: what matters

  • The tester needs a Google account with the invited email, and App Tester. This is where most people get stuck: "I did not get the email" (spam) or "I accepted with another account".
  • Each release has a version and a build: two uploads with the same versionCode are the same release. Bump the +N before every build.
  • Feedback: in App Tester the tester uses the feedback button or shakes the phone; it arrives with a screenshot in the release's "Feedback" tab.
  • With the workflow, the tag v0.3.0 triggers build and distribution. Nobody's laptop is the source of the builds any more, and the tests run first.
  • One Firebase project per group. Do not invite the class to the project; invite them as testers.

Teacher demo: TestFlight live

  1. Xcode → Runner → Signing & Capabilities: team and bundle id (pt.uac.tempoacores). With "Automatically manage signing", Xcode creates the certificate and the profile.
  2. Build: flutter build ipa --release creates build/ios/archive/Runner.xcarchive and the .ipa in build/ios/ipa/. It takes 5 to 8 minutes.
  3. Upload: open the .xcarchive in Xcode → "Distribute App" → App Store Connect → Upload; or the Transporter app with the .ipa. Processing takes 5 to 15 minutes and an email arrives.
  4. App Store Connect → TestFlight: the build shows up. If it asks for "Missing Compliance", that is the question about encryption; with ITSAppUsesNonExemptEncryption set to false in Info.plist it stops asking.
  5. Internal testers: group "Team", add App Store Connect users; available immediately. External: public link; the first build of each version goes to Beta App Review.
  6. On the iPhone: TestFlight app → accept → install. Feedback with a screenshot arrives in App Store Connect.
No Mac? Codemagic compiles and uploads with your certificate on one of their machines. It is the alternative for iOS without Apple hardware; you still need the paid account.
