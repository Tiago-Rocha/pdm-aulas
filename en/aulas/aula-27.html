Workshop: signing the app and getting it to testers

Theory (30 minutes): why you distribute early, the three routes to testers and what signing an app means. Workshop: your group's app signed, in a GitHub Release and on three classmates' phones through Firebase App Distribution. Then: icon, launch screen, dark theme and two languages.

No lesson repository: everything happens in the group projectBreak at 10:20
Plan: 08:30 to 09:00 theory; 09:00 to 10:20 steps 1 to 3 (app identity, key, release APK); 10:20 to 10:35 break; 10:35 to 10:50 GitHub Release; 10:50 to 11:35 Firebase and testers; 11:35 to 12:15 testing and feedback as issues, and polish for whoever finishes; 12:15 to 12:30 final round, each group shows its Release and how many testers have the app. At the start: anyone with an iPhone hears now that today they test on a classmate's Android phone or in the emulator. The testers are the classmates from the other groups, so each person tests the app of at least one group that is not their own. Testers need a Google account on their phone, and the group needs a computer with the Android SDK working (the one they already use to run the app).

By the end of this lesson you can

  • Say what only shows up when the app reaches someone else's phone.
  • Choose between Firebase App Distribution, Google Play internal testing and TestFlight.
  • Explain what an app signature is and what happens if the key is lost.
  • Build a signed release APK, with the right version, in a GitHub Release.
  • Get your group's app onto three classmates' phones and turn what they say into issues.

For the tests: you distribute early to find what the emulator and flutter run hide, before the deadline.

What to say: until today the app has only run with the cable plugged in, in debug mode, in the hands of the people who made it. Today it leaves home. The first row is the one that catches most people: in debug, Flutter adds the Internet permission on its own; in a release build it only exists if it is in the main AndroidManifest.xml. For the tests: App Distribution and TestFlight come before the store; internal testing is already in the store.

What to say: all three do the same job, which is getting a build onto a tester's phone without going through the public store. App Distribution is the only one that covers both platforms and it is free, so it is today's. On iOS even it needs the paid Apple account, because the build has to be signed with a profile that lists each tester's iPhone. That is why anyone with an iPhone tests today on a classmate's Android phone or in the emulator. In TestFlight, team testers (up to 100) get the build straight away; external testers (up to 10,000) go through an Apple review, usually a day. Google Play internal testing has no long review and the app installs through the Play Store itself, with automatic updates, but it needs the developer account. Builds expire: 90 days in TestFlight, 150 in App Distribution.

Signing: the app says who made it

KeyWhere it livesWhat for
DebugOne per computer, from the Android SDKflutter run
Group upload keyA .jks file with a password, outside gitBuilds for testers and for the store
  • The phone only accepts an update signed with the same key as the installed app.
  • Each computer has its own debug key: a classmate's build on top gives "App not installed".
  • Lost key: testers uninstall, install again and lose their data.

For the tests: an app only updates with the same key; the .jks and its password never go into the repository.

What to say: the signature is how Android knows that version 2 comes from whoever made version 1. Without it, anyone could publish an "update" of someone's banking app. The debug key lives in ~/.android/debug.keystore and is created on the first build, one per computer: that is why, in a group, a classmate's APK will not install on top of yours. The store adds one more layer: Google keeps the app's final key (Play App Signing) and you only hold the upload key, so a lost upload key can be reset through support. Outside the store, like today, nobody can recover it. A .jks in a commit counts as published, even if it is deleted right after.

Two things that identify the app

FieldExampleRule
applicationIdpt.uac.pdm.tempo_acores.group1Unique, never changes once published
versionName0.3.0What the person reads in settings
versionCode3A whole number that always goes up
  • In pubspec.yaml, version: 0.3.0+3 gives both versions: before and after the +.
  • The four apps in the class have the same applicationId today: to the phone, they are the same app.

For the tests: the versionCode has to go up with every build you distribute; Android refuses a lower version on top of a higher one.

What to say: the applicationId is the app's name on the phone and in the store, for example com.whatsapp or com.spotify.music. Two apps with the same id cannot live side by side: a tester with one group's app installed cannot install another group's (different signatures) and, if the key were the same, one would replace the other. So in step 1 each group adds its own suffix: lowercase letters, numbers and _, starting with a letter. The versionName is free (some teams use dates); the versionCode is what the system compares. Starting at 0.3.0 says it is the third sprint and not yet version 1.

Step 1: the id, the version and .gitignore

applicationId = "pt.uac.pdm.tempo_acores.group1"
  • Only this line. The namespace, further up, stays as it is.
  • The suffix is yours: lowercase letters, numbers and _, starting with a letter. It does not change again.
  • In pubspec.yaml: version: 1.0.0+1 becomes version: 0.3.0+3.

What to say: the applicationId is registered in Firebase in step 5 and cannot be changed there afterwards, so choose it calmly now (someone's surname, the name you gave the app). Changing the namespace would mean moving the MainActivity.kt folder: do not touch it. Going down from 1.0.0 to 0.3.0 is fine, because Android only compares the number after the +, and 3 is bigger than 1.

Step 2: creating the group key

$kt = "C:\Program Files\Android\Android Studio\jbr\bin\keytool.exe"
& $kt -genkey -v -keystore C:\dev\upload-keystore.jks `
    -keyalg RSA -keysize 2048 -validity 10000 -alias upload
  • Copy the three lines in one go. The VS Code terminal is PowerShell; cmd will not work.
  • The key lives in C:\dev, outside the project folder: it cannot end up in a commit.
  • On macOS: "/Applications/Android Studio.app/Contents/jbr/Contents/Home/bin/keytool" and -keystore ~/upload-keystore.jks.
What to say: one person in the group creates the key, then passes the file and the password to the rest of the group through a private channel. If Android Studio is somewhere else, flutter doctor -v shows it on the "Java binary at" line: it is that folder, with keytool instead of java. Plain keytool, without the path, says "is not recognized" on Windows and "Unable to locate a Java Runtime" on a Mac. The backtick at the end of the second line is PowerShell's line continuation and must be the last character: with a space after it, the command breaks in half. On macOS it is the backslash. The key goes to C:\dev and not the home folder because a user name with an accent (João) breaks the path in key.properties. If there is no C:\dev: mkdir C:\dev. -validity 10000 is about 27 years: the Play Store requires the key to last at least until 2033. The alias upload is the name of the key inside the file.

Step 2: key.properties, outside git

storePassword=the-group-password
keyPassword=the-group-password
keyAlias=upload
storeFile=C:/dev/upload-keystore.jks
  • Created in VS Code, in the android/ folder, not in android/app/. Notepad saves it as key.properties.txt.
  • On Windows, forward slashes: C:/dev/.... On macOS: /Users/ana/upload-keystore.jks.
  • Both passwords are the same.
  • git status does not show it: android/.gitignore already hides it.
What to say: this is where Gradle gets the path and the password; the code holds no secret. Each person who makes release builds has their own key.properties, with the path on their own computer. With single backslashes, Java reads the \u in \upload as a Unicode code and the build stops with "Malformed \uxxxx encoding"; elsewhere the backslash just vanishes. Forward slashes work on Windows and need nothing else. Tested with Android Studio's Java. If git status shows key.properties, someone touched .gitignore: stop and call me. In GitHub Actions CI this file does not exist, and that is fine: CI only runs analyze and test.

Step 2: Gradle reads key.properties

import java.util.Properties
import java.io.FileInputStream

plugins { ... }  // stays as it is

val keystoreProperties = Properties()
val keystorePropertiesFile = rootProject.file("key.properties")
if (keystorePropertiesFile.exists()) {
    keystoreProperties.load(FileInputStream(keystorePropertiesFile))
}

The two import lines before plugins; the five new lines between plugins and android {.

What to say: rootProject is the android/ folder, so the file is android/key.properties. The if is what lets classmates without the key keep working: flutter run still works, and Gradle only complains when someone asks for a release build. Imports in the middle of the file give "Unresolved reference: Properties".

Step 2: the key, after defaultConfig

signingConfigs {
    create("release") {
        keyAlias = keystoreProperties.getProperty("keyAlias")
        keyPassword = keystoreProperties.getProperty("keyPassword")
        storeFile = keystoreProperties.getProperty("storeFile")
            ?.let { file(it) }
        storePassword = keystoreProperties.getProperty("storePassword")
    }
}

It goes between the end of defaultConfig { } and buildTypes {, which is already there.

What to say: this only gives the key a name, "release", and says where it is. It does not sign anything yet: the next slide connects it to the release build. The ?.let is what avoids the error when there is no key.properties.

Step 2: release now uses the group key

buildTypes {
    release {
        signingConfig = signingConfigs.getByName("release")
    }
}
  • Before it said getByName("debug"): release was signed with each computer's debug key.
  • The two comment lines with TODO above it can go.
  • Commit, PR and merge of steps 1 and 2. git status shows four files.
What to say: this is where the magic happens. The Flutter template comment said "Signing with the debug keys for now, so flutter run --release works". Now release uses the group key, and anyone in the group's APK installs over anyone else's. This file, with these changes, was tested on the four class projects and the release build passes on all of them.

Step 3: the release APK

# the app calls the API with pdm.json
flutter build apk --release --dart-define-from-file=pdm.json
# the app does not call the API yet, or has the clientId in the code
flutter build apk --release
  • It takes 3 to 10 minutes. It ends up in build\app\outputs\flutter-apk\app-release.apk.
  • With the phone plugged in: flutter install. Then, without the cable: sign in, islands, trails, airplane mode.
What to say: what goes into the APK is public, even the clientSecret: anyone with the file can read it. In a real app, secrets stay on the server. The PostHog key you saw on Wednesday is public for that very reason: it only lets you send events. The release build compiles Dart to machine code for three architectures: it is faster and smaller than debug, and has no DEBUG banner. pdm.json is Wednesday's, at the project root, with CLIENT_ID and CLIENT_SECRET. Without --dart-define-from-file, in the groups that use String.fromEnvironment, the clientId is empty and login gives 401. With the flag and no file, the build stops straight away. On Windows, the first build may take longer because the antivirus scans Gradle. Drag the APK from that folder, in File Explorer, to GitHub and Firebase. No Android phone: flutter install also installs on the emulator, or drag the APK onto the emulator window.

  1. On the phone, the Firebase App Distribution email (check spam): "Get started", Google account, accept.

Icon and launch screen

flutter_launcher_icons:
  android: true
  ios: true
  image_path: assets/icon.png
flutter_native_splash:
  color: "#0B4F6C"
  image: assets/logo.png
  • Install: flutter pub add --dev flutter_launcher_icons flutter_native_splash
  • Generate: dart run flutter_launcher_icons and dart run flutter_native_splash:create
What to say: both packages generate native files (the mipmap and drawable folders on Android, Assets.xcassets on iOS) from one image. The generated files go into the commit; whoever makes the next build does not need to run anything. The icon must be square, with no transparency for iOS; on Android 8 or later the system crops it into a circle or a rounded square, so keep the drawing away from the edges (the adaptive_icon_foreground option does it properly). The launch colour is the main colour of the design system. On Android 12 or later the launch screen is always the icon in the centre: the package handles that.

Dark theme and two languages

MaterialApp.router(
  theme: AppTheme.light,
  darkTheme: AppTheme.dark,
  themeMode: ThemeMode.system,
  localizationsDelegates: AppLocalizations.localizationsDelegates,
  supportedLocales: AppLocalizations.supportedLocales,
  routerConfig: router,
)
  • AppTheme is your design system's theme, in light and in dark.
  • The strings: lib/l10n/app_pt.arb and app_en.arb; on screen, AppLocalizations.of(context)!.islands.
What to say: with ThemeMode.system the app follows the phone, no button needed. A dark theme is not inverted colours: it is the same ColorScheme with brightness: Brightness.dark, and with ColorScheme.fromSeed Flutter generates both from the same colour. For languages: flutter_localizations (from the SDK) and intl in pubspec.yaml, generate: true in the flutter section, and an l10n.yaml with arb-dir: lib/l10n and template-arb-file: app_pt.arb. flutter pub get generates the AppLocalizations class and the import is package:tempo_acores/l10n/app_localizations.dart. Island names and text that comes from the API are not translated.
