Workshop: signing the app and getting it to testers

Theory (30 minutes): why you distribute early, the three routes to testers and what signing an app means. Workshop: your group's app signed, in a GitHub Release and on three classmates' phones through Firebase App Distribution. Then: icon, launch screen, dark theme and two languages.

No lesson repository: everything happens in the group projectBreak at 10:20
Plan: 08:30 to 09:00 theory; 09:00 to 10:20 steps 1 to 4 (app identity, key, release APK, GitHub Release); 10:20 to 10:35 break; 10:35 to 11:30 Firebase and testers; 11:30 to 12:15 feedback as issues and polish; 12:15 to 12:30 final round, each group shows its Release and how many testers have the app. At the start: anyone with an iPhone hears now that today they test on a classmate's Android phone or in the emulator. The testers are the classmates from the other groups, so each person tests the app of at least one group that is not their own. Testers need a Google account on their phone, and the group needs a computer with the Android SDK working (the one they already use to run the app).

By the end of this lesson you can

  • Say what only shows up when the app reaches someone else's phone.
  • Choose between Firebase App Distribution, Google Play internal testing and TestFlight.
  • Explain what an app signature is and what happens if the key is lost.
  • Build a signed release APK, with the right version, in a GitHub Release.
  • Get your group's app onto three classmates' phones and turn what they say into issues.

What only shows up on someone else's phone

What changesWhat tends to break
Release buildNo Internet permission in the manifest: nothing loads
Another phoneClipped text, buttons off screen, large font
First timeEmpty screen without an account, permissions denied
School networkError and loading states nobody has seen
Another mindPaths through the app the group never thought of

For the tests: you distribute early to find what the emulator and flutter run hide, before the deadline.

What to say: until today the app has only run with the cable plugged in, in debug mode, in the hands of the people who made it. Today it leaves home. The first row is the one that catches most people: in debug, Flutter adds the Internet permission on its own; in a release build it only exists if it is in the main AndroidManifest.xml. The app opens, the login fails and it looks like an API error. Some groups in this room have this problem, and we fix it in step 1. Large font: many people have the system font size at maximum, and that is where Rows overflow. "Another mind" is what automated tests never catch: the person taps where nobody expected.

Three routes to testers

App DistributionGoogle PlayTestFlight

TodayYesLater onNo: Mac and paid account

For the tests: App Distribution and TestFlight come before the store; internal testing is already in the store.

What to say: all three do the same job, which is getting a build onto a tester's phone without going through the public store. App Distribution is the only one that covers both platforms and it is free, so it is today's. On iOS even it needs the paid Apple account, because the build has to be signed with a profile that lists each tester's iPhone. That is why anyone with an iPhone tests today on a classmate's Android phone or in the emulator. In TestFlight, team testers (up to 100) get the build straight away; external testers (up to 10,000) go through an Apple review, usually a day. Google Play internal testing has no long review and the app installs through the Play Store itself, with automatic updates, but it needs the developer account. Builds expire: 90 days in TestFlight, 150 in App Distribution.

Signing: the app says who made it

KeyWhere it livesWhat for
DebugOne per computer, from the Android SDKflutter run
Group upload keyA .jks file with a password, outside gitBuilds for testers and for the store
  • The phone only accepts an update signed with the same key as the installed app.
  • Each computer has its own debug key: a classmate's build on top gives "App not installed".
  • Lost key: testers uninstall, install again and lose their data.

For the tests: an app only updates with the same key; the .jks and its password never go into the repository.

What to say: the signature is how Android knows that version 2 comes from whoever made version 1. Without it, anyone could publish an "update" of someone's banking app. The debug key lives in ~/.android/debug.keystore and is created on the first build, one per computer: that is why, in a group, a classmate's APK will not install on top of yours. The store adds one more layer: Google keeps the app's final key (Play App Signing) and you only hold the upload key, so a lost upload key can be reset through support. Outside the store, like today, nobody can recover it. A .jks in a commit counts as published, even if it is deleted right after.

Two things that identify the app

FieldExampleRule
applicationIdpt.uac.pdm.tempo_acores.group1Unique, never changes once published
versionName0.3.0What the person reads in settings
versionCode3A whole number that always goes up
  • In pubspec.yaml, version: 0.3.0+3 gives both versions: before and after the +.
  • The four apps in the class have the same applicationId today: to the phone, they are the same app.

For the tests: the versionCode has to go up with every build you distribute; Android refuses a lower version on top of a higher one.

What to say: the applicationId is the app's name on the phone and in the store, for example com.whatsapp or com.spotify.music. Two apps with the same id cannot live side by side: a tester with one group's app installed cannot install another group's (different signatures) and, if the key were the same, one would replace the other. So in step 1 each group adds its own suffix: lowercase letters, numbers and _, starting with a letter. The versionName is free (some teams use dates); the versionCode is what the system compares. Starting at 0.3.0 says it is the third sprint and not yet version 1.

What to say: steps 1 to 4 before the break; 5 and 6 after it. One person in the group does steps 1 to 4 on the computer where the app already runs, and the others follow and prepare the tester list and the release notes. Steps 1 and 2 are one PR (without the key.properties or the .jks, of course). Today's goal is step 5 done by every group; the polish is the bonus. In the final round each group shows its Release and a phone from outside the group with the app.

Step 1: the app with its own name

<manifest xmlns:android="http://schemas.android.com/apk/res/android">
    <uses-permission android:name="android.permission.INTERNET" />
    <application
        android:label="Tempo Açores · Group 1"
        ...
  • The Internet permission: without it, the release build never reaches the API.
  • The label is the name under the icon. Today all four apps are called tempo_acores.
  • In android/app/build.gradle.kts: applicationId = "pt.uac.pdm.tempo_acores.group1".
  • In pubspec.yaml: version: 0.3.0+3.
What to say: three files, four lines. Only the applicationId changes; the namespace and the MainActivity.kt folder stay as they are. The suffix is the group's choice (someone's surname, the name they gave the app), in lowercase. Anyone who had the app installed with the old id ends up with two on the phone: uninstall the old one. The location permissions already there stay the same. pubspec.yaml comes with 1.0.0+1; going down to 0.3.0 is fine, because Android only compares the number after the +. Before moving on to step 2, run flutter run to check nothing broke.

Step 2: the group key

keytool -genkey -v -keystore ~/upload-keystore.jks \
        -storetype JKS -keyalg RSA -keysize 2048 \
        -validity 10000 -alias upload
  • It asks for a password and a name. Put the password somewhere safe for the group straight away.
  • The file stays in your home folder, outside the project: there is no way it ends up in a commit.
  • One person creates it; the .jks and the password go to the rest of the group through a private channel.
  • On Windows: keytool is in the Java folder that flutter doctor -v shows.
What to say: -validity 10000 is about 27 years; the Play Store requires the key to last at least until 2033. The alias upload is the key's name inside the file. On Windows, in PowerShell: keytool -genkey -v -keystore $env:USERPROFILE\upload-keystore.jks -storetype JKS -keyalg RSA -keysize 2048 -validity 10000 -alias upload. If it says "keytool is not recognized", run flutter doctor -v, copy the path after "Java binary at", swap the java at the end for keytool and put the path in quotes. Keep the password in two places (a password manager and a private group note). Do not write it in the class WhatsApp group.

Step 2: key.properties, outside git

storePassword=the-group-password
keyPassword=the-group-password
keyAlias=upload
storeFile=/Users/ana/upload-keystore.jks
  • On Windows, double backslashes: storeFile=C:\\Users\\ana\\upload-keystore.jks
  • android/.gitignore already has key.properties and *.jks: git status does not show them.
  • Each person who makes release builds has their own key.properties, with the path on their computer.
What to say: this is where Gradle gets the path and the passwords; the code has no secret in it. If the keystore asked for the same password twice, both lines are the same. Before committing the PR, run git status and check this file is not listed. If it is, someone changed the .gitignore: stop and call me. In GitHub Actions CI this file does not exist, and that is fine: CI only runs analyze and test.

Step 2: Gradle reads the key

import java.util.Properties
import java.io.FileInputStream
// the plugins { } block stays here, as it is

val keystoreProperties = Properties()
val keystorePropertiesFile = rootProject.file("key.properties")
if (keystorePropertiesFile.exists()) {
    keystoreProperties.load(FileInputStream(keystorePropertiesFile))
}

Without key.properties nothing breaks: flutter run keeps working for anyone who does not have it.

What to say: rootProject is the android/ folder, so the file is android/key.properties. The if is what lets classmates without the key keep working: Gradle only complains when someone asks for a release build. Common mistake: pasting the imports in the middle of the file gives "Unresolved reference: Properties" or a syntax error in plugins.

Step 2: signing with the group key

signingConfigs {
    create("release") {
        keyAlias = keystoreProperties.getProperty("keyAlias")
        keyPassword = keystoreProperties.getProperty("keyPassword")
        storeFile = keystoreProperties.getProperty("storeFile")
            ?.let { file(it) }
        storePassword = keystoreProperties.getProperty("storePassword")
    }
}

Right after it, in the buildTypes that already exists, the "debug" line becomes signingConfig = signingConfigs.getByName("release").

What to say: this is where the magic happens. The buildTypes in the Flutter template signed the release with the debug key, "so flutter run --release works", says the comment. Now the release uses the group key. Common errors: "Keystore was tampered with, or password was incorrect" is the wrong password in key.properties; "Keystore file not found" is the storeFile path (on Windows, the double backslashes); "SigningConfig release is missing required property" is a key.properties that is not in android/.

Step 3: the release APK

flutter build apk --release --dart-define-from-file=pdm.json
flutter install
  • It ends up in build/app/outputs/flutter-apk/app-release.apk.
  • Without --dart-define-from-file, the clientId is empty and login returns 401.
  • flutter install puts this APK on the connected phone. Then, without the cable: log in, islands, trails, flight mode.
  • What goes in the APK is public: anyone with the file can read the clientSecret. In a real app, secrets stay on the server.
What to say: the first release build takes a few minutes, because it compiles Dart to machine code for three architectures. It is faster and smaller than debug, and has no banner. If you get "INSTALL_FAILED_UPDATE_INCOMPATIBLE", the phone has an app with the same id and a different key: uninstall it. If the app opens and nothing loads, it is almost always the Internet permission from step 1. The last line ties back to what we saw with PostHog: a key that ships inside an app is public. That is why the PostHog key only lets you send events, and why real APIs do not trust what the app says without a login.

Step 4: a GitHub Release

gh release create v0.3.0 \
  build/app/outputs/flutter-apk/app-release.apk \
  --title "0.3.0" --notes "First version for testers"
  • Or on the website: Releases, "Draft a new release", tag v0.3.0, drag the APK in.
  • The tag matches pubspec.yaml: 0.3.0+3 gives v0.3.0.
  • Each new build for testers: new version, new Release. It is requirement 11 of the project.
What to say: the Release keeps the exact APK the testers got, next to the commit it came from. When a tester says "in 0.3.0 Corvo does not open", you know what code was there. Release notes are written for the testers: what is new and what you want them to try. If gh is not installed or logged in, the website does the same in two minutes. The tag is created on the latest main commit: merge the PR from steps 1 and 2 first.

Step 5: Firebase App Distribution

  1. console.firebase.google.com: new project tempo-acores-group1, without Google Analytics.
  2. Add an Android app with the applicationId from step 1. The google-services.json and the SDK are for another day.
  3. In the menu, App Distribution: drag in app-release.apk.
  4. The emails of three classmates from outside the group, release notes and "Distribute".
What to say: to distribute you do not need to put anything from Firebase inside the app; the project only stores the builds and the testers. The applicationId has to be exactly the one from step 1 and cannot be changed once registered. Add your group mates to the project (Project settings, Users and permissions) so everyone can upload builds. If the menu does not show App Distribution, search for that name in the console search bar. For later builds there is a command-line shortcut: firebase appdistribution:distribute app-release.apk --app <App ID> --testers "a@x.pt,b@y.pt" --release-notes "...". The App ID is in the project settings and starts with 1:.

Step 5: on the tester's side

  1. An email arrives from Firebase App Distribution. Accept the invitation with a Google account.
  2. Download the app from the page that opens. Android asks to allow installs from the browser.
  3. Create a new account in the app and use it for ten minutes, with no help from whoever made it.

With an iPhone: test on a classmate's Android phone or in the emulator.

What to say: the invitation may land in spam or in Gmail's Promotions tab. It can be accepted with any Google account, not necessarily the one it was sent to. If Android refuses the install, it is the "Install unknown apps" option for Chrome, in settings; turning it off again afterwards is good practice. "No help from whoever made it" is the hardest rule: the person who made the app stays quiet, watches and takes notes, like in a usability test. That is where you see where people get lost.

Step 6: feedback you can fix

Phone: Samsung A52, Android 14 · version 0.3.0
Did: opened Corvo and tapped Trails
Expected: the list of trails on Corvo
Got: blank screen, no "Try again"
Screenshot: attached
  • The tester sends this to the group, with the screenshot; the group opens the issue with the tester label.
  • The repositories are private: testers do not see your code, and do not need to.
  • Each person tests the app of at least one other group.
What to say: "it doesn't work" cannot be fixed; "I did this, expected that, got something else" can. The version matters: what was broken in 0.3.0 may already be fixed in 0.3.1. Issues with the tester label go into the sprint 3 backlog like any other, and at the review you show the ones you closed. A group whose app is still far behind gets feedback anyway: the tester says what they saw, even if it is not much.

Icon and launch screen

flutter_launcher_icons:
  android: true
  ios: true
  image_path: assets/icon.png
flutter_native_splash:
  color: "#0B4F6C"
  image: assets/logo.png
  • Install: flutter pub add --dev flutter_launcher_icons flutter_native_splash
  • Generate: dart run flutter_launcher_icons and dart run flutter_native_splash:create
What to say: both packages generate native files (the mipmap and drawable folders on Android, Assets.xcassets on iOS) from one image. The generated files go into the commit; whoever makes the next build does not need to run anything. The icon must be square, with no transparency for iOS; on Android 8 or later the system crops it into a circle or a rounded square, so keep the drawing away from the edges (the adaptive_icon_foreground option does it properly). The launch colour is the main colour of the design system. On Android 12 or later the launch screen is always the icon in the centre: the package handles that.

Dark theme and two languages

MaterialApp.router(
  theme: AppTheme.light,
  darkTheme: AppTheme.dark,
  themeMode: ThemeMode.system,
  localizationsDelegates: AppLocalizations.localizationsDelegates,
  supportedLocales: AppLocalizations.supportedLocales,
  routerConfig: router,
)
  • AppTheme is your design system's theme, in light and in dark.
  • The strings: lib/l10n/app_pt.arb and app_en.arb; on screen, AppLocalizations.of(context)!.islands.
What to say: with ThemeMode.system the app follows the phone, no button needed. A dark theme is not inverted colours: it is the same ColorScheme with brightness: Brightness.dark, and with ColorScheme.fromSeed Flutter generates both from the same colour. For languages: flutter_localizations (from the SDK) and intl in pubspec.yaml, generate: true in the flutter section, and an l10n.yaml with arb-dir: lib/l10n and template-arb-file: app_pt.arb. flutter pub get generates the AppLocalizations class and the import is package:tempo_acores/l10n/app_localizations.dart. Island names and text that comes from the API are not translated.
