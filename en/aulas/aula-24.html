Inside the phone

Which sensor will your project app use, and which permission does it ask for?

  • Motion: accelerometer, gyroscope, magnetometer and barometer are chips a few millimetres wide on the board.
  • Face: Face ID, proximity and ambient light sit in the front module, right against the screen.
  • Back: the wireless charging coil and the NFC antenna are the same sheet.
  • Each sensor has its own permission, or none, and its own battery cost.
  • The measurements are those of the iPhone 17 Pro; the drawing is generic.
This slide is the map: after it each sensor has its own slide and its own figure. Let the figure open by itself and go through the sensors; then pick with the buttons or with the up and down arrow keys (the side ones still change slides). Expected answers: location, for the weather where they are, which asks on both platforms; camera, if there are photos, which also asks. If someone says accelerometer, ask what for: it asks for nothing, but it costs battery. The iPhone 17 Pro is the one you will show later on.

Before you drop it: while falling, what does the accelerometer read? More than 1 g, 1 g, or almost 0?

Give them two minutes to open the page and tap Turn on sensors. On an iPhone a request for motion access appears: it is a permission, the same idea as the permissions table later on. Ask before they drop: they vote with their fingers (1, 2 or 3), discuss in pairs for a minute and vote again; only then do they drop. Answer: almost 0. In free fall the phone falls with gravity and the chip feels nothing, like an astronaut on the space station. Lying on the table it reads 1 g because the table pushes it up. The page measures how long the force stays near zero and works out the height: half of g times the time squared. Challenge: who gets 50 cm. Always onto a jacket, backpack or cushion, never the floor. Then, for each sensor, they go back to its tab. Spin is the gyroscope: it only measures how fast it turns, and the turns are that speed added up over time, so the error piles up. Sound is the microphone: it asks for a separate permission, and the dB are rough because the microphone is not calibrated; leave it for the end, to wake the room up. The page was made in HTML and JavaScript with DeviceMotionEvent, Geolocation and getUserMedia: the same numbers sensors_plus, geolocator and a microphone package give you in Flutter. If the room network fails, use phyphox on your iPhone (Live slide). To show your iPhone on the projector: wireless, open Control Centre, tap Screen Mirroring and pick the Mac (AirPlay). Both must be on the same network; if the university Wi-Fi does not allow it, connect the Mac to the iPhone hotspot. This is best for Drop and Spin, because a cable pulls the phone. With a cable: QuickTime, File, New Movie Recording, and pick the iPhone in the menu next to the record button. Analogy The sensors are the phone's senses: the accelerometer and gyroscope are the inner ear, the camera is sight, NFC is touch, BLE is the voice.
What to say: the accelerometer also measures gravity, so at rest it reads 1 g. Steps show up in Health and Google Fit; shake to undo is also the accelerometer. The gyroscope is behind video stabilisation, Instagram augmented reality filters and the Spin tab in the lab. The barometer gives relative altitude and could give the local pressure in Tempo Açores. sensors_plus exposes each sensor as a Stream: accelerometerEventStream(), gyroscopeEventStream(), magnetometerEventStream(), barometerEventStream(). The accelerometer, gyroscope and magnetometer ask for no permission; the barometer, on iOS, asks for Motion & Fitness. A stream at 100 Hz keeps the CPU awake. Choose the samplingPeriod, cancel the subscription in dispose and when the app goes to the background. In the analogy: the inner ear gives balance and movement without asking anyone's leave; NFC is touch because it only works within centimetres and in an instant; BLE is the voice because it reaches tens of metres and holds a conversation. Each sense has its own permission and its own cost in battery. Quick question: which app on their phone uses the gyroscope without asking for anything? Almost every camera app.

Inside the accelerometer

With the phone lying still on the table, what does z read? And if we drop it?

  • A mass held by springs: when the phone sets off, it lags behind; when it brakes, it moves forward.
  • The chip only feels changes in speed, not speed: at constant speed, x goes back to 0.
  • The mass moves by nanometres and is measured by the capacitance between the combs. The figure exaggerates it hugely.
  • Real world: fall detection on the Apple Watch, car crash detection on the iPhone and the Pixel, step counting.
  • In the lab: the Drop and Level tabs, both are this chip.
Let the figure run on its own for a few seconds, then take the mouse: when the carriage sets off to the right, the mass lags behind and x goes up; when it brakes, the mass moves forward and x goes negative. Answers: lying still, z reads 9.8, because gravity pulls the mass against the springs just like an upward acceleration; in free fall all three axes read close to 0, because the mass falls together with the chip and stops pulling on the springs.

Magnetometer: the phone's compass

Why does Google Maps sometimes ask you to wave the phone in a figure 8?

  • It measures the Earth's magnetic field on three axes. It is weak: about 44 µT in the Azores.
  • When the phone turns, the field stays put and x and y change. That gives the heading.
  • Magnets, magnetic cases, MagSafe, speakers and nearby metal throw the reading off. Inside cars and steel-framed buildings the arrow also goes wrong.
  • It asks for no permission on either platform.
  • Real world: the arrow in Google Maps, the Compass app, Sky Guide.
  • In the lab: Compass tab. Turn slowly, then put the phone next to the laptop.
For the magnet demo: press 270° West and then M; facing East the deflection is small. Answer: turning the phone in every direction lets the system work out which part of the field comes from the device itself (magnets, the case, MagSafe) and subtract it. That is calibrating.

Barometer: which floor you are on

The pressure also changes with the weather outside. Why does the Health app not count floors when a cold front arrives?

  • Inside the chip there is a thin membrane over an almost empty cavity, and the air pushes on it.
  • The higher you go, the less air above and the less it bends: one floor, 3 m, is about 0.36 hPa less.
  • Real world: flights climbed in the Health app, altitude in Strava, the floor in an emergency call, the weather forecast.
  • Permission: on iOS, relative altitude asks for Motion and fitness (NSMotionUsageDescription). On Android it is read without asking.
  • Some cheap phones have no barometer.
For the demo: move the pointer over the building or use the up and down keys. Answers: a weather front changes the pressure slowly, about 1 hPa per hour; one floor changes it by 0.36 hPa in a few seconds. The phone compares with the pressure from a moment ago and only counts fast changes, and at the same time the accelerometer sees steps. The app only counts a floor when both agree.

GPS: the phone only listens

Why do Android and iOS call this permission "location" and not "GPS"?

  • Each satellite sends only the exact time and where it is.
  • The phone measures how long the signal took and turns that into a distance to each one.
  • With four satellites it fixes its own clock and knows where it is, within about 5 m. A millionth of a second off in the clock is 300 m.
  • GPS sends nothing and needs no internet: it works in airplane mode. Only the map needs data.
  • In the lab: GPS tab. Compare the accuracy in the middle of the room and by the window.
The third step of the figure shows why four satellites are needed. Answers: indoors the satellite signal barely gets through, and the system uses the Wi-Fi networks and cell towers around you, within about 25 m and with less battery. That is why there is approximate and precise location: ACCESS_COARSE_LOCATION and ACCESS_FINE_LOCATION on Android, and the Precise Location switch on iOS.

The camera and the LiDAR

The iPhone 17 Pro has three cameras on the back. When you zoom from 1x to 4x, what changes?

  • A camera is a stack of lenses on top of a grid of 48 million pixels, and each pixel only sees one colour.
  • The LiDAR fires infrared light and measures how long it takes to come back: that gives the distance to each point.
  • Both use the same permission, the camera one.
  • The LiDAR brings its own light: portrait mode and the Measure app work in the dark.
  • Real world: IKEA Place, Polycam, the Measure app.
In the LiDAR part, move the pointer over the sofa, the table and the wall to see each distance. Answers: most of the time it switches cameras, from the main one to the 4x telephoto; in between it crops and enlarges the image, and since all three are 48 MP the crop loses little.

NFC: it only works up close

Why does a transport card work for years without ever being charged?

  • The phone creates a magnetic field at 13.56 MHz, and the tag answers with a few bytes: an NDEF message.
  • The range of about 4 cm is on purpose: nobody pays by accident from across the room. To pay, you have to tap.
  • Real world: Apple Pay, Google Pay, door cards, museum labels.
  • The phone's antenna is the coil under the back, next to the wireless charging one.
Answers: it has no battery. The card's coil picks up the reader's magnetic field and that is enough to power the chip and send the answer. Away from a reader it is completely off.

NDEF: text, URL or contact on a tag. Tags in museums, business cards, stickers that open an app.

For the tests: in NFC, Android does reading, writing and HCE; iOS reads and writes in a system session and HCE is a special case.

What to say: NDEF is the common language of tags (text, URL, vCard, Wi-Fi configuration); in Flutter you read and write with nfc_manager. On Android, android.permission.NFC in the manifest is enough, with no prompt. iOS reads since 11 and writes since 13, always in a session with the system sheet; it needs NFCReaderUsageDescription in Info.plist and the capability in Xcode. HCE (Host Card Emulation) is the phone pretending to be a card, with a native service. Payments (Apple Pay, Google Wallet) use NFC with a Secure Element: an ordinary app cannot imitate that, a home-made "transport card" is not possible. Show the "Ready to Scan" sheet on your iPhone; Android users find it odd that there is no silent mode. Frequent question: "can I make a gym access card?" Only if the reader accepts NDEF or HCE; transport cards require a Secure Element and agreements with the operator.

NFC: how an app reads a tag

  • Where you see it: tags in museums, business cards, stickers that open an app, NFC automations in Shortcuts on the iPhone.
  • For the tests: before reading, check that there is NFC; on the iPhone reading only happens with the "Ready to Scan" sheet open, and it closes by itself after 60 seconds.
What to say: this is the sequence of any app that reads tags. In Flutter, with nfc_manager: isAvailable() first, because without NFC, or with NFC turned off in the settings, the session never opens (show a message, not an error); then startSession with an onDiscovered that receives the tag; read the NDEF message and finally stopSession. The vibration (HapticFeedback.heavyImpact()) is the feedback: the person feels the read before looking at the screen. On Android the read happens with nothing on screen; on the iPhone the "Ready to Scan" sheet appears and the session closes by itself after 60 seconds. Setup: android.permission.NFC in the manifest; on iOS, NFCReaderUsageDescription in Info.plist and the "Near Field Communication Tag Reading" capability in Xcode. Detail: in a text record the first bytes are the status and the language code (pt, en), so the app skips them before showing the text. Question for the class: "a bank card has NFC; could your app read it?" No: it carries no NDEF, and the iPhone does not let a normal app talk to payment cards. Anyone who wants to try can buy NTAG215 stickers and write them with the NFC Tools app.

Bluetooth Low Energy

RoleWhat it doesExamples
CentralScans and connectsThe phone
PeripheralAdvertises that it existsMi Band, AirTag, lock, weather station

Flow: scan, connect, discover services, read or subscribe to characteristics.

Permissions: up to Android 11 the scan asks for location; from 12 it asks for Bluetooth; iOS asks for a text in Info.plist.

For the tests: BLE on old Android asks for location in order to scan; it is not a bug, it is privacy.

What to say: the phone can also be a peripheral, but it is rarely worth it. Each characteristic is a UUID with bytes; the meaning comes from the standard (Heart Rate Service is 0x180D, the same on every wristband) or from the manufacturer. In Flutter you use flutter_blue_plus: scan with filters, connection, read(), write() and lastValueStream per characteristic. Permissions: on Android 12 or newer they are BLUETOOTH_SCAN and BLUETOOTH_CONNECT; with the neverForLocation flag the app promises it does not use the scan to find out where you are and no longer asks for location. On iOS it is NSBluetoothAlwaysUsageDescription. The pandemic contact tracing system was BLE: each phone advertised an identifier that changed every so often. In the analogy BLE is the voice: it reaches tens of metres and keeps up a conversation, unlike NFC, which is a touch. Question for the class: why did a Bluetooth app ask for location? Answer: because beacons sit in fixed places (shops, museums, airports), and whoever sees the beacons knows where you are.

Inside Face ID

A banking app uses Face ID. What does the app learn about your face?

  • The phone does not take a photo of your face: it throws over 30,000 infrared dots and measures where they land.
  • It works in the dark thanks to the infrared illuminator.
  • A photo cannot fool it: it has no relief, and the dots stay in a straight grid.
  • Apple says the chance of someone else unlocking it is 1 in 1,000,000.
  • In Flutter: local_auth. On iOS it needs NSFaceIDUsageDescription and the system asks the first time; on Android, USE_BIOMETRIC in the manifest is enough, with no prompt.
Answers: nothing. The 3D map is compared in the Secure Enclave, a separate chip, your face never leaves it, and the app only gets yes or no.

No: it proves it to the phone, and the server still only sees the token.

Proximity and ambient light

Why does the screen turn off during a call, but not when you watch a video with the phone close to your face?

  • Near the top of the screen there is an infrared emitter and a receiver: if the light comes back, something is less than 3 cm away and the screen turns off.
  • Next to them, a photodiode measures the light in lux, and the brightness follows: 5 in a dark room, 300 in an office, 30,000 in the sun.
  • Real world: in WhatsApp, when you play a voice message with the phone at your ear, the sound switches to the earpiece and the screen goes dark.
  • On iOS no app can read the lux, there is no API. On Android they can be read without asking.
Answers: the proximity sensor is only on when needed, during a call or when an app asks for it (on iOS, isProximityMonitoringEnabled; that is what WhatsApp does). If it were always on, it would drain the battery and turn the screen off by mistake, in a pocket or reading in bed.

A flashlight app asks for your location. Suspicious? And why does the barometer ask on iOS while the accelerometer does not?

Answers: a flashlight does not need to know where you are; when an app asks for more than it needs, it is almost always to sell data. Real case: in 2013 the US FTC took action against Brightest Flashlight, an Android flashlight, for sending its users' location to advertisers. The barometer: Apple treats altitude and floors climbed as physical activity, like steps, and since iOS 17.4 it asks for the Motion & Fitness authorisation. The accelerometer is still free, but not harmless: from Android 12 its rate is capped at 200 Hz for privacy reasons. The iPhone does have an ambient light sensor, but Apple does not give them to apps; on Android they can be read without asking.

Live: the sensors of an iPhone 17 Pro

  • The phyphox app (free, from RWTH Aachen University) shows each sensor as a graph: the same numbers sensors_plus gives you in a Stream.
  • Lying on the table: the accelerometer z reads 9.8; x and y stay near 0.
  • Falling onto a cushion: all three axes go to 0 during the fall.
  • Spinning on the table: the gyroscope rises while it spins and goes back to 0 when it stops.
  • A magnet next to it: the magnetometer jumps and the compass loses north.
  • From the floor to the ceiling: the pressure drops by about 0.3 hPa, roughly 0.12 hPa per metre.

The first time phyphox reads the barometer, iOS asks for Motion & Fitness: that is the barometer row in the previous table.
