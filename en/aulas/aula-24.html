Inside the phone

Question for the class: which sensor will your project app use, and which permission does it ask for?

This slide is the map: after it each sensor has its own slide and its own figure. Let the figure open by itself and go through the sensors; then pick with the buttons or with the up and down arrow keys (the side ones still change slides). Notice that all the motion sensing fits in chips a few millimetres wide on the board, and that what sees your face sits in the front module, right against the screen. The wireless charging coil and the NFC antenna are the same sheet under the back. Expected answers: location, for the weather where they are, which asks on both platforms; camera, if there are photos, which also asks. If someone says accelerometer, ask what for: it asks for nothing, but it costs battery. The measurements are those of the iPhone 17 Pro you will show later on; the drawing is generic.

Question for the class: before you drop it: while falling, what does the accelerometer read? More than 1 g, 1 g, or almost 0?

Give them two minutes to open the page and tap Turn on sensors. On an iPhone a request for motion access appears: it is a permission, the same idea as the permissions table later on. Ask before they drop: they vote with their fingers (1, 2 or 3), discuss in pairs for a minute and vote again; only then do they drop. Answer: almost 0. In free fall the phone falls with gravity and the chip feels nothing, like an astronaut on the space station. Lying on the table it reads 1 g because the table pushes it up. The page measures how long the force stays near zero and works out the height: half of g times the time squared. Challenge: who gets 50 cm. Always onto a jacket, backpack or cushion, never the floor. Then, for each sensor, they go back to its tab. The page was made in HTML and JavaScript with DeviceMotionEvent and Geolocation: the same numbers sensors_plus and geolocator give you in Flutter. If the room network fails, use phyphox on your iPhone (Live slide).

  • sensors_plus exposes each sensor as a Stream: accelerometerEventStream(), gyroscopeEventStream(), magnetometerEventStream(), barometerEventStream(). The accelerometer, gyroscope and magnetometer ask for no permission; the barometer, on iOS, asks for Motion & Fitness.
  • A stream at 100 Hz keeps the CPU awake. Choose the samplingPeriod, cancel the subscription in dispose and when the app goes to the background.

For the tests: motion sensors do not ask for permission (the barometer on iOS is the exception), but they cost battery; cancel the stream when nobody is looking.

Analogy The sensors are the phone's senses. The accelerometer and the gyroscope are the inner ear: they give balance and movement without asking anyone's leave. The camera is sight. NFC is touch: it only works within centimetres and in an instant. BLE is the voice: it reaches tens of metres and holds a conversation. Each sense has its own permission and its own cost in battery.

Inside the accelerometer

Question for the class: with the phone lying still on the table, what does z read? And if we drop it?

Let the figure run on its own for a few seconds, then take the mouse: when the carriage sets off to the right, the mass lags behind and x goes up; when it brakes, the mass moves forward and x goes negative; at constant speed, x goes back to 0. The chip only feels changes in speed, not speed. Answers: lying still, z reads 9.8, because gravity pulls the mass against the springs just like an upward acceleration; in free fall all three axes read close to 0, because the mass falls together with the chip and stops pulling on the springs. Fall detection on the Apple Watch and car crash detection on the iPhone and the Pixel start from the accelerometer. The shift of the mass is hugely exaggerated: in the real chip it is nanometres, measured by the capacitance between the combs. In the lab: the Drop and Level tabs, both are this chip.

Magnetometer: the phone's compass

Question for the class: why does Google Maps sometimes ask you to wave the phone in a figure 8?

What to say: the chip measures the Earth's magnetic field on three axes, and it is weak: about 44 µT in the Azores. When the phone turns, the field stays put and x and y change; that gives the heading behind the arrow in Google Maps, the Compass app and Sky Guide. For the magnet demo: press 270° West and then M; facing East the deflection is small. Answers: magnets, magnetic cases, MagSafe, speakers and nearby metal throw the reading off. Turning the phone in every direction lets the system work out which part of the field comes from the device itself and subtract it: that is calibrating. Inside cars and buildings with a steel structure the arrow also goes wrong. It asks for no permission on either platform. In the lab: Compass tab. Turn slowly, then put the phone next to the laptop.

Barometer: which floor you are on

Question for the class: the pressure also changes with the weather outside. Why does the Health app not count floors when a cold front arrives?

What to say: inside the chip there is a thin membrane over an almost empty cavity, and the air pushes on it. The higher you go, the less air above and the less it bends: one floor, 3 m, is about 0.36 hPa less. For the demo: move the pointer over the building or use the up and down keys. Answers: a weather front changes the pressure slowly, about 1 hPa per hour; one floor changes it by 0.36 hPa in a few seconds. The phone compares with the pressure from a moment ago and only counts fast changes, and at the same time the accelerometer sees steps. The app only counts a floor when both agree. Real examples: flights climbed in the Health app, altitude in Strava, the floor in an emergency call and the weather forecast. Permission: on iOS, reading relative altitude asks for Motion and fitness (NSMotionUsageDescription); on Android the pressure sensor is read without asking, but some cheap phones have no barometer.

GPS: the phone only listens

Question for the class: why do Android and iOS call this permission "location" and not "GPS"?

What to say: each satellite sends only the exact time and where it is. The phone measures how long the signal took and turns that into a distance to each one; with four satellites it fixes its own clock and knows where it is, within about 5 m. Answers: indoors the satellite signal barely gets through, and the system uses the Wi-Fi networks and cell towers around you, within about 25 m and with less battery. That is why there is approximate and precise location: ACCESS_COARSE_LOCATION and ACCESS_FINE_LOCATION on Android, and the Precise Location switch on iOS. Bonus: GPS sends nothing and needs no internet, so it works in airplane mode; only the map needs data. The third step shows why four are needed: a millionth of a second off in the phone clock is 300 m. In the lab: GPS tab. Compare the accuracy in the middle of the room and by the window.

The camera and the LiDAR

Question for the class: the iPhone 17 Pro has three cameras on the back. When you zoom from 1x to 4x, what changes?

What to say: a camera is a stack of lenses on top of a grid of 48 million pixels, and each pixel only sees one colour. The LiDAR fires infrared light and measures how long it takes to come back; both use the same permission, the camera one. In the LiDAR part, move the pointer over the sofa, the table and the wall to see each distance. Answers: most of the time it switches cameras, from the main one to the 4x telephoto; in between it crops and enlarges the image, and since all three are 48 MP the crop loses little. Bonus question: why do portrait mode and the Measure app work in the dark with LiDAR? Because the LiDAR brings its own infrared light. Apps: IKEA Place, Polycam, the Measure app.

Camera and gallery

  • image_picker opens the system camera or gallery and returns a file. It is enough for most cases: a profile photo, attaching an image to a form. It is what WhatsApp does when you attach a photo.
  • camera gives you the live preview inside the app, control of focus, flash and video. You need it to read QR codes or apply filters. It costs more code and more permissions.
  • Permissions: Android 13+ uses the Photo Picker and does not ask for permission to choose from the gallery; the camera requires CAMERA. iOS requires NSCameraUsageDescription and NSPhotoLibraryUsageDescription in Info.plist, with a justification text, or the app crashes.
  • Size: a 12 MP photo is 4 to 6 MB. Before sending, shrink it with maxWidth and imageQuality from image_picker or with flutter_image_compress. A 6 MB upload on 3G fails; a 300 KB one does not.

For the tests: image_picker delegates to the system and has no preview; camera has a preview and costs more permissions and code.

Ask: for an app to report potholes in the road, image_picker or camera? Answer: image_picker, they only need the photo. Insist on compression: anyone who has had uploads fail on a weak network gets it straight away.

NFC: it only works up close

Question for the class: why does a transport card work for years without ever being charged?

What to say: the tag has no battery: the phone's field wakes it up and gives it the energy to answer. The range of about 4 cm is on purpose: nobody pays by accident from across the room. Answers: it has no battery. The card's coil picks up the reader's magnetic field, at 13.56 MHz, and that is enough to power the chip and send a few bytes back (on a phone, an NDEF message). Away from a reader it is completely off. Apple Pay and Google Pay, door cards and museum labels work the same way. The short range is a security choice: to pay, you have to tap. The phone's antenna is the coil you saw under the back, next to the wireless charging one.

NFC

  • NDEF tags are the common language of tags: text, URL, vCard, Wi-Fi configuration. Reading and writing with nfc_manager. Examples: tags in museums, business cards, stickers that open an app.
  • Android: reads and writes tags in the background, with no system screen; it needs android.permission.NFC in the manifest, with no prompt to the user. It does HCE (Host Card Emulation): the phone pretends to be a card, with a native service.
  • iOS: reads since iOS 11 and writes since 13, but always in a session with the system sheet visible ("Ready to Scan"); it needs NFCReaderUsageDescription and the capability in Xcode. HCE only since iOS 17.4, only in the EU and with Apple's authorisation.
  • Payments (Apple Pay, Google Wallet) use NFC with a Secure Element. An ordinary app cannot imitate that; a home-made "transport card" is not possible.

For the tests: in NFC, Android does reading, writing and HCE; iOS reads and writes in a system session and HCE is a special case.

Show the "Ready to Scan" sheet on your iPhone; Android users find it odd that there is no silent mode. Frequent question: "can I make a gym access card?" Only if the reader accepts NDEF or HCE; transport cards require a Secure Element and agreements with the operator.

Bluetooth Low Energy

  • Central is whoever scans and connects (the phone). Peripheral is whoever advertises (the wristband, the temperature sensor, the lock). The phone can also be a peripheral, but it is rarely worth it.
  • Flow: scan for advertisements, connect, discover services and, finally, read, write or subscribe to characteristics. Each characteristic is a UUID with bytes; the meaning comes from the standard (Heart Rate Service 0x180D) or from the manufacturer.
  • flutter_blue_plus: scan with filters, connection, read(), write() and lastValueStream per characteristic.
  • Permissions: up to Android 11 the scan requires location permission, because beacons reveal where you are. Android 12+ has BLUETOOTH_SCAN and BLUETOOTH_CONNECT, with the neverForLocation flag. iOS asks for NSBluetoothAlwaysUsageDescription.
  • Examples: Mi Band, home weather stations, AirTags, the contact tracing system from the pandemic.

For the tests: BLE on old Android asks for location in order to scan; it is not a bug, it is privacy.

Inside Face ID

Question for the class: a banking app uses Face ID. What does the app learn about your face?

What to say: the phone does not take a photo of your face: it throws over 30,000 infrared dots and measures where they land. That is why it works in the dark and a photo is not enough; your face never leaves the Secure Enclave. Answers: nothing. The 3D map is compared in the Secure Enclave, a separate chip, and the app only gets yes or no. A photo cannot fool it because it has no relief: the dots stay in a straight grid. It works in the dark thanks to the infrared illuminator. Apple says the chance of someone else unlocking it is 1 in 1,000,000. In Flutter it is local_auth: on iOS it needs NSFaceIDUsageDescription and the system asks the first time; on Android, USE_BIOMETRIC in the manifest is enough, with no prompt.

Biometrics, haptics, home screen widgets

  • local_auth: Face ID, Touch ID, fingerprint, with authenticate(localizedReason: ...). It unlocks something local: showing the stored token, entering a sensitive screen. It does not replace authentication on the server.
  • Haptics: HapticFeedback.lightImpact(), mediumImpact(), heavyImpact(), selectionClick(). No permission, no package, and it is one of the details that most make an app feel native. iOS has the Taptic engine; on Android it depends on the hardware.
  • Home screen widgets: home_widget bridges to App Widgets (Android) and WidgetKit (iOS). The widget's UI is native; Flutter only writes the data. The perfect case: today's forecast for the favourite location.
  • Live Activities (iOS 16.1+) and Dynamic Island in one sentence: a live notification whose data you update; Swift code is mandatory. Examples: deliveries, flights, sports results.

For the tests: biometrics is local unlocking; a home screen widget is drawn in native code and Flutter only gives it data.

Ask the class where a haptic would make sense in Tempo Açores. Good answers: when adding a favourite, when completing the pull-to-refresh, when a red warning arrives. Common question: "does biometrics prove to the server who I am?" No: it proves it to the phone, and the server still only sees the token.

Proximity and ambient light

Question for the class: why does the screen turn off during a call, but not when you watch a video with the phone close to your face?

What to say: near the top of the screen there is an infrared emitter and a receiver: if the light comes back, something is less than 3 cm away and the screen turns off. Next to them, a photodiode measures the light in lux, and the brightness follows. Answers: the proximity sensor is only on when needed, during a call or when an app asks for it (on iOS, isProximityMonitoringEnabled). If it were always on, it would drain the battery and turn the screen off by mistake, in a pocket or reading in bed. Real example: in WhatsApp, when you play a voice message and hold the phone to your ear, the sound switches to the earpiece and the screen goes dark. Lux: 5 in a dark room, 300 in an office, 30,000 in the sun. On iOS no app can read it, there is no API; on Android it can be read without asking.

Question for the class: if a sensor is missing, what does your app do?

What to say: the accelerometer costs cents and every phone has one; the money goes on the screen, the processor and the cameras. The 150 € column is what often happens, not always. On paper almost every phone has "GPS, camera, sensors"; the difference is the quality of each part and the ones that are missing. Camera: cheap phones add 2 MP macro or depth lenses that are barely useful, so the box can say "three cameras". Dual-frequency GPS (L1 and L5): the signal bouncing off buildings fools it less, and the arrow on the map does not jump streets. Barometer: many low and mid-range Androids do not have one, so they do not count floors. Gyroscope: some very cheap phones lack it, and augmented reality and 360 videos do not work or work badly. Face: face unlock with the normal camera can be fooled by a photo, so Android classes it as weak biometrics and banking apps ask for the PIN or a finger; Face ID projects more than 30,000 dots and measures the face in 3D, and it is good enough to pay. Proximity: many cheap phones use a virtual sensor (ultrasound from the earpiece or software), and sometimes the screen lights up during a call and the ear presses buttons. Vibration: a motor with a spinning weight buzzes; the linear motor (the Taptic Engine on the iPhone) gives short, crisp taps, the haptics we talked about. Ultra-wideband (UWB): measures the distance to another device to the centimetre; it is used to find an AirTag and for digital car keys. Before the question, each student says which rows their phone has (the lab shows what the browser gives; on Android, phyphox lists every sensor). If the sensor is missing, the app must not crash or keep waiting: check first, and hide the feature or show an alternative. That is what the lab's Compass tab does when there is no compass.

Question for the class: a flashlight app asks for your location. Suspicious? And why does the barometer ask on iOS while the accelerometer does not?

Answers: a flashlight does not need to know where you are; when an app asks for more than it needs, it is almost always to sell data. Real case: in 2013 the US FTC took action against Brightest Flashlight, an Android flashlight, for sending its users' location to advertisers. The barometer: Apple treats altitude and floors climbed as physical activity, like steps, and since iOS 17.4 it asks for the Motion & Fitness authorisation. The accelerometer is still free, but not harmless: from Android 12 its rate is capped at 200 Hz for privacy reasons. The iPhone does have an ambient light sensor, but Apple does not give them to apps; on Android they can be read without asking.

Live: the sensors of an iPhone 17 Pro

  • The phyphox app (free, from RWTH Aachen University) shows each sensor as a graph: the same numbers sensors_plus gives you in a Stream.
  • Lying on the table: the accelerometer z reads 9.8; x and y stay near 0.
  • Falling onto a cushion: all three axes go to 0 during the fall.
  • Spinning on the table: the gyroscope rises while it spins and goes back to 0 when it stops.
  • A magnet next to it: the magnetometer jumps and the compass loses north.
  • From the floor to the ceiling: the pressure drops by about 0.3 hPa, roughly 0.12 hPa per metre.

The first time phyphox reads the barometer, iOS asks for Motion & Fitness: that is the barometer row in the previous table.

Demo: read an NFC tag and vibrate

On the teacher's phone. The code is short, and what matters is the sequence: check availability, open a session, handle the tag, close the session.

import 'package:flutter/services.dart';
import 'package:nfc_manager/nfc_manager.dart';
import 'package:nfc_manager_ndef/nfc_manager_ndef.dart';

class NfcService {
  Future<void> readTag({required void Function(String) onRead}) async {
    if (!await NfcManager.instance.isAvailable()) {
      throw StateError('NFC unavailable or turned off on this phone');
    }
    await NfcManager.instance.startSession(
      pollingOptions: {
        NfcPollingOption.iso14443,
        NfcPollingOption.iso15693,
      },
      onDiscovered: (NfcTag tag) async {
        final message = await Ndef.from(tag)?.read();
        final record = message?.records.firstOrNull;
        // payload.skip(3): skips the Text Record header
        final text = record == null
            ? 'tag with no NDEF content'
            : String.fromCharCodes(record.payload.skip(3));
        await HapticFeedback.heavyImpact();
        onRead(text);
        await NfcManager.instance.stopSession();
      },
    );
  }
}

Demo: what matters

  • isAvailable() first: without NFC, or with NFC turned off in the settings, the session never opens. Show a message, not a crash.
  • On Android, onDiscovered fires silently. On iOS the system sheet appears and the session closes by itself after 60 seconds.
  • The payload of a Text Record starts with a status byte and the language code (pt, en): hence the skip(3).
  • Configuration: <uses-permission android:name="android.permission.NFC"/> in AndroidManifest.xml; on iOS, NFCReaderUsageDescription in Info.plist and the "Near Field Communication Tag Reading" capability in Xcode.
  • The HapticFeedback.heavyImpact() is the feedback: the user feels that the read happened before reading the screen.
Exercise 1 If your project has a gesture that deserves feedback (adding a favourite, finishing the pull-to-refresh, receiving a red warning), open a five-line PR with HapticFeedback.selectionClick() or mediumImpact() at that point. Test on the phone, not on the emulator: the emulator does not vibrate.
