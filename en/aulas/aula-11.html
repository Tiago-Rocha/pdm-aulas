Accessibility and mini-project

Part one: accessibility in mobile, at a high level, and a video. Part two: carry on with the Trilhos mini-project, which is due tomorrow.

Two-hour lesson. Reference times: theory and video until 09:15; mini-project until the end. The mini-project is still due tomorrow, Wednesday, at 16:00. Accessibility does not count towards the mini-project grade, but applying it to the Trilhos app is a good extra.

By the end of this lesson you can

  • Explain why accessibility is mandatory and what ignoring it costs.
  • Recognise who benefits: permanent, temporary and situational disabilities.
  • Name the four rules that matter most in an app: large text, screen reader, buttons with a name, colour and contrast.
  • Test an app with TalkBack or VoiceOver and with the text at maximum.

Why

  • Law: the European Accessibility Act has been in force since June 2025. Shopping, banking, transport and service apps in the EU must be accessible.
  • Stores: Apple and Google review accessibility, and Apple shows "Accessibility Nutrition Labels" in the App Store.
  • People: about 1 in 6 people has some disability. And we all use our phones in the sun, one-handed, or with the system text enlarged.
  • Engineering: an accessible app has named widgets and a clear structure. It is also easier to test and maintain.
Ask the class Who has their phone text larger than normal? What about your parents or grandparents?
Answers to the questions. Usually several hands go up, and almost everyone has a relative with the text at maximum. That is the point: it is not a rare case, it is everyday life. In Android settings the text goes up to 200%; on iOS, with the accessibility sizes, even more.

Designing for the permanent case solves the other two. An app that works one-handed and with your eyes closed works for everyone.

This table comes from Microsoft's "Inclusive Design Toolkit". Ask the class which row they were in this week: almost everyone has been in the situational column (sun, one hand, noise). Video captions are the classic example: designed for deaf people, and today most people watch videos with the sound off.

Flutter does a lot on its own: Text, buttons and fields already have semantics. You handle what is missing.

Colour example from the mini-project: a trail's difficulty has a coloured dot and the text "Fácil". Colour alone would not do, because about 8% of men are colour-blind. Touch targets: IconButton and ListTile already have 48 dp; a GestureDetector around a 20 dp icon does not.

How to test

  • On the phone: TalkBack on Android (Settings, Accessibility) or VoiceOver on iPhone (triple-click the side button). With the reader on, tap once to hear and twice to activate.
  • Large text: in the phone settings, font size at maximum.
  • In Flutter: showSemanticsDebugger: true on MaterialApp draws over each area what the screen reader reads. It works in Chrome.
Ask the class Five minutes with your eyes closed and TalkBack on: what do you think will go worst?
Answers to the questions. Usually: icons without a name ("button, button, button"), images that say the file name or nothing, and cards read in pieces ("Seg", "23 degrees") with no sense that they belong together. These are the three problems in the previous table. If you have an Android at hand, demo it with TalkBack: it is the moment that sticks.
